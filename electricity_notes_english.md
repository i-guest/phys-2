# CHAPTER 1: ELECTRIC FORCES (PDF pp. 8-)

## p.9 - Biography box
Charles Coulomb (1736-1806), French physicist, who established experimentally the force law that carries his name. *(Figure: engraved portrait.)*

## pp.10-11 - 1.1 Electric charge
- Static electricity has been known since antiquity (rubbing amber). Rubbing only separates two kinds of charge that were already in the neutral body.
- Convention: **positive** charge is what a glass rod keeps after rubbing with silk; the silk gets an equal negative charge.
- Atom: nucleus with **protons** (+) and **neutrons** (neutral), with **electrons** (-) orbiting. The electron charge e is the fundamental unit. SI unit of charge: **coulomb (C)**.
- Charge is **quantized** (Faraday, Thomson, Millikan oil-drop experiment): **q = N e**, N = 0, 1, 2, ... (Eq. 1.1)

| Particle | Charge | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| Electron | -1.6x10^-19 C | 9.109534x10^-31 kg |
| Proton | +1.6x10^-19 C | 1.6726485x10^-27 kg |
| Neutron | 0 | 1.6749543x10^-27 kg |

## p.12 - Example 1.1
Electrons lost for a charge of +1.44x10^-8 C: N = q/e = 9x10^10 electrons.

## pp.12-14 - 1.2 Conservation of charge
- Charge is neither created nor destroyed; it is only transferred (the rod and silk charges sum to zero).
- Same family as conservation of mass-energy and momentum. Example: electron + positron annihilate into energy (E = mc^2), and the total charge stays 0 before and after.
- **Example 1.2:** balance charge in the reaction H(1,1) + Be(9,4) -> X + n. The neutron is neutral, so X has charge 5 (boron, B-9).

## pp.14-17 - 1.3 Coulomb's law
- The four fundamental forces: strong (nuclear, very strong, very short range), electromagnetic, weak (about 10^-13 of nuclear; beta emission), gravitational (about 10^-39 of the electric force, but infinite range).
- Coulomb (1784): force is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. Doubling the distance cuts the force to 1/4.

**F = k q1 q2 / r^2** (1.2), with k = 8.98755x10^9, about 9.0x10^9 N m^2 C^-2

**F = (1 / 4 pi eps0) q1 q2 / r^2** (1.3), with the permittivity constant eps0 = 8.9x10^-12 C^2/(N m^2)

- The force acts along the line joining the charges. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. *(Figures 1.1a and 1.1b: two charges with force arrows pointing apart for repulsion and toward each other for attraction.)*
- Using signed charges: F > 0 means repulsion, F < 0 means attraction. The two forces are equal and opposite (Newton's third law).
- Several charges acting on q0: **superposition**. Compute each force separately, then add them as vectors: F = F01 + F02 + ... + F0n.

## p.18 - Example 1.3
q1 = 2.5 uC, q2 = 0.7 uC, r = 12 cm gives F = 1.1 N (repulsive).

## pp.18-19 - Example 1.4
Two protons 1 mm apart: electric repulsion = 2.3x10^-22 N. Gravitational attraction (G = 6.672x10^-11, m = 1.67x10^-27 kg) = 1.86x10^-58 N, which is negligible next to the electric force.

## pp.20-22 - Example 1.5 (vector sum of forces)
- Set-up *(Fig. 1.2)*: q4 = +1 uC at the centre B of a "T". q1 = -4 uC at A (above, distance r = 10 cm), q2 = +2 uC at C (right), q3 = +5 uC at D (left).
- F41 = 3.6 N, attractive, toward A. F42 = 1.8 N, repulsive, pointing left. F43 = 4.5 N, repulsive, pointing right.
- x direction: 4.5 - 1.8 = 2.7 N toward C. y direction: 3.6 N toward A.
- Resultant *(Fig. 1.3)*: |F| = sqrt(2.7^2 + 3.6^2) = **4.5 N**, at theta = tan^-1(3.6/2.7) = **53.1 degrees** from the x axis.

## Example 7 conclusion (p.30)
F03 = 7.2 N (along OC, repulsive from C's side) opposes F01 = 3.6 N, giving 3.6 N toward the F03 side. F04 = 7.2 N (along DO) opposes F02 = 3.6 N, giving 3.6 N toward the F04 side. The two net forces are perpendicular, so **F = sqrt(3.6^2 + 3.6^2) = 5.1 N**, along the bisector of angle COB.

## Example 8 (pp.30-31)
- Two small identical **conducting** spheres (conductor: free electrons, e.g. silver or copper; insulator: electrons bound, e.g. glass or ebonite) are 25 cm apart and attract with 0.2 N. They are touched briefly and put back 25 cm apart, and now repel with 0.09 N. Find the initial charges.
- Before contact: k q1 q2 / r^2 = 0.2 N, so |q1 q2| = 1.4x10^-12 C^2 (the product is negative).
- After contact each sphere carries (q1 + q2)/2, so k ((q1 + q2)/2)^2 / r^2 = 0.09 N, which gives |q1 + q2| = 1.58x10^-6 C. *(The book prints "q1 - q2" here; with q2 signed, it is the sum that is meant.)*
- Solving the two equations together: **q1 = +/-0.63 uC and q2 = -/+2.21 uC** (opposite signs).

# CHAPTER 2: THE ELECTRIC FIELD (PDF pp.38-)

## p.38 - Chapter title page: "Chapter Two - The Electric Field".
## p.39 - Biography box
James (Clerk) Maxwell, born 1831 in Edinburgh, Scotland; died 1879. He derived the famous Maxwell equations and summarized his study of electricity and magnetism in his book on electricity and magnetism ("A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism"). *(Figure: engraved portrait.)*

## pp.40-41 - Introduction and 2.1 Electric field intensity
- A charge acts on another with no contact ("action at a distance"). Coulomb's law gives only the final force. The field picture explains it: a charge changes the surrounding space, and a second charge placed there responds to that **electric field**. *(Footnote: this book treats only fields of static charges; fields of moving charges are a separate "field".)*
- Faraday first proposed the field idea, which was strange to earlier scientists, Newton included (he disliked action at a distance for gravity). In Faraday's picture the field of a charge spreads in all directions.
- To measure it, place a small positive **test charge** q0 at the point. It must be small enough not to disturb the field being measured.

**E = F / q0** (2.1), units **N/C**

- E is a vector. It points in the direction of the force on a positive test charge, that is, the direction q0 would move if free.

**Example 2.1:** a 2x10^-9 C charge feels 1.4x10^-6 N. E = F/q0 = **700 N/C**. *(p.42)*

## pp.42-44 - 2.2 Lines of force
- *Fig. 2.1:* field lines of an isolated positive charge radiate straight out in all directions, symmetrically. By convention they start on positive charges and end on negative charges (for an isolated positive charge, the negative charge is "at infinity").
- Field lines never cross. They repel one another sideways, as if there were a repulsive force between them.
- The direction of E at a point is the **tangent to the field line** at that point. *(Fig. 2.2: field lines of two equal and opposite charges, which leave the + charge and curve around into the - charge. A point P is marked with the tangent arrow E.)*
- Near a charge, where the force is large, the lines are close together and dense; they thin out with distance. The magnitude of E is proportional to the number of lines crossing a unit area perpendicular to them.
- *Fig. 2.3:* two equal positive charges. The lines bend away from each other, and the field nearly vanishes midway between the charges (the lines almost disappear there).
- *Fig. 2.4:* two parallel plates with equal and opposite charge. The lines between the plates are straight, parallel and evenly spaced, so the **field there is uniform**.

## p.45 - 2.3 Calculating the electric field (start)
Place a test charge q0 at distance r from a source charge q. Coulomb gives F = (1/4 pi eps0) q q0 / r^2. Dividing by q0:

**E = F / q0 = (1/(4 pi eps0)) q / r^2** (2.2)

- E at a point depends on the source charge q and on the distance r to the field point, not on q0.
- Direction: radially **outward** from a positive q, radially **inward** toward a negative q.
- A free positive q0 moves along E (away from a positive q). *(The explanation of the negative case continues on p.46.)*

## pp.46-47 - Several charges and continuous charge
- A negative source pulls a free positive q0 toward it, against the field direction of a positive source. This is what Coulomb's law already told us.
- For several charges q1, q2, ..., qn the force on q0 is the vector sum of the individual forces, so the field is too:

**E = E1 + E2 + ... + En = sum of En** (2.3) - the **principle of superposition**. Find each charge's field at the point separately, then add as vectors.

- For a **continuous** charge distribution (on a sphere, wire, etc.) the sum becomes an integral:

**E = integral of dE** (2.4), with **dE = (1/4 pi eps0) dq / r^2**, where the charge is split into small pieces dq.

## pp.47-49 - Example 2.2 (field of two point charges at a point)
- Set-up *(Fig. 2.5)*: right triangle. A (-8 uC) and B (+6 uC) are 10 cm apart on a horizontal line. P is 5 cm directly above B, so AP = 11.18 cm. Find E at P.
- Field of +6 uC: E6 = (9x10^9)(6x10^-6)/(0.05)^2 = **2.16x10^7 N/C**, along BP (upward, the +y direction). Components: Ex = 0, Ey = +2.16x10^7.
- Field of -8 uC: E(-8) = (9x10^9)(8x10^-6)/(0.1118)^2 = **0.576x10^7 N/C**, directed from P toward A (it points toward the negative charge). *(Fig. 2.6 shows both vectors at P with the angle phi marked.)*
  - Ex = -E(-8) sin(phi) = -0.576x10^7 (10/11.18) = -0.515x10^7 N/C
  - Ey = -E(-8) cos(phi) = -0.576x10^7 (5/11.18) = -0.258x10^7 N/C
- Totals: Ex = -0.515x10^7 N/C, Ey = (2.16 - 0.258)x10^7 = 1.9x10^7 N/C.
- **E = sqrt(Ex^2 + Ey^2) = 1.97x10^7 N/C**, at **theta = tan^-1(Ey/Ex) = 105.2 degrees**, measured from the positive x axis.

## pp.50-51 - Example 2.3 (electric dipole field on the perpendicular bisector)
- An **electric dipole** is two equal and opposite charges, +q and -q, separated by a very small distance 2a compared with the distance to the field point.
- Set-up *(Fig. 2.7)*: -q above and +q below, each a distance a from the midpoint. P is at distance r from the midpoint along the perpendicular bisector. E+ and E- have equal magnitude (1/4 pi eps0) q/(a^2 + r^2), and each makes angle theta with the line through P.
- The components along the bisector cancel, and the components parallel to the dipole axis add. Each contributes E cos(theta), with cos(theta) = a/sqrt(a^2 + r^2):

**E = (1/4 pi eps0) 2 q a / (a^2 + r^2)^(3/2)**

- For r >> a we may neglect a^2 next to r^2:

**E = (1/4 pi eps0) 2 q a / r^3 = (1/4 pi eps0) p / r^3**

- **Dipole moment p = 2qa** (a vector, units C m). Far from a dipole the field falls off as **1/r^3**, faster than the 1/r^2 of a single charge.

## pp.52-53 - Example 2.4 (dipole field on its axis)
- Set-up *(Fig. 2.8)*: the dipole lies along a vertical line, with -q nearer to P and +q farther. P is at distance r from the midpoint, so -q is at r - a and +q at r + a.
- E from -q = (1/4 pi eps0) q/(r - a)^2, pointing down toward -q. E from +q = (1/4 pi eps0) q/(r + a)^2, pointing up away from +q. The two oppose each other:

E = (1/4 pi eps0) [ q/(r-a)^2 - q/(r+a)^2 ] = (1/4 pi eps0) 4 q a r / (r^2 - a^2)^2 = (1/2 pi eps0) p r / (r^2 - a^2)^2

- For r >> a this reduces to **E = (1/2 pi eps0) p / r^3** (Eq. 2.6). *(The scan appears to print r^2 in the denominator; the line above gives r^3, which is the correct far-field law.)* On the axis the dipole field is **twice** its value at the same distance on the perpendicular bisector.

## pp.53-55 - Example 2.5 (infinite line of charge)
- An infinitely long straight wire carries uniform **linear charge density lambda (C/m)**. Find E at distance x from the wire. *(Fig. 2.9: vertical wire; a small element dy at height y; field point P at perpendicular distance x with the line r from dy to P. dE has components dEx and dEy, and theta is the angle between r and the perpendicular x.)*
- Element charge dq = lambda dy, so dE = (1/4 pi eps0) lambda dy / (x^2 + y^2). Components: dEx = dE cos(theta), dEy = dE sin(theta), with cos(theta) = x / sqrt(x^2 + y^2).
- Ex = (lambda x / 4 pi eps0) times the integral from -infinity to +infinity of dy/(x^2 + y^2)^(3/2). That integral equals 2/x^2 (appendix table), so

**E = lambda / (2 pi eps0 x)**, directed perpendicular to the wire.

- The y component is the integral of y dy/(x^2 + y^2)^(3/2) over a symmetric range, which is **zero**. Physically, each element above the point is cancelled by an equal one below it.

## pp.55-56 - 2.4 A charge in an electric field
A charge q of mass m in a **uniform** field E feels a constant force, so it has uniform acceleration:

**a = F/m = (q/m) E** (2.7), in the direction of E for a positive charge.

Starting from rest: v = a t, distance s = (1/2) a t^2, and kinetic energy (1/2) m v^2 = **q E s** (the work done by the field).

## pp.56-57 - Example 2.6 (Millikan oil drop)
- An oil droplet is held in equilibrium between two charged plates. m = 2.8x10^-15 kg and E = 2.45x10^4 N/C. *(Fig. 2.10: a charge in a uniform field drawn as parallel field lines.)*

## p.57 - 2.5 Cathode-ray tube (start)
Televisions and oscilloscopes use electron-beam deflection in a cathode-ray tube. Electrons are emitted from a heated filament F *(Fig. 2.11)*, accelerated toward the screen S by the anode A, and reach point d *(Fig. 2.12)* with speed vx, entering between four mutually perpendicular deflection plates. *(Continues on the next page.)*

## pp.58-60 - 2.5 Cathode-ray tube (continued)
- Without a field between the plates, electrons travel straight and hit the middle of the screen, making a bright spot. *(Fig. 2.11: the tube. Filament F on the left, anode A, vertical-deflection plates Y, horizontal-deflection plates Z, and screen S at the wide end. Axes x (along the tube), y (up), z.)* A field between the plates deflects the beam.
- Take a field Ey between the Y plates *(Fig. 2.12: electron enters at point d with speed vx, travels plate length l, leaves at P with vertical displacement yp, then travels a further horizontal distance D to the screen, where the total deflection is ys)*:
  1. a_y = (e/m) E_y (acceleration inside the plates)
  2. y_p = (1/2) a t_p^2
  3. t_p = l / v_x (the horizontal speed is constant because no force acts along x)
  4. y_p = (1/2)(e/m) E_y l^2 / v_x^2
  5. At the exit the vertical speed is v_p = a_y t_p.
- After the plates the electron moves in a straight line for t_s = D/v_x, so y_s = y_p + v_p t_s. Substituting:

**y_s = (1/2)(e/m) E_y l^2/v_x^2 + (e/m) E_y l D / v_x^2**

- This is the path equation: the deflection on the screen is proportional to Ey. vx is set by the potential difference between anode and filament (see Chapter 4). The same analysis applies to the Z plates, with the field along z. *(Footnote: potential difference is defined in Chapter 4.)*

## p.61 - 2.6 Dipole in an electric field (torque and work)
- The field exerts a torque on the dipole, trying to turn it toward the field. In vector form: **tau = p x E** (Eq. 2.9).
- The torque magnitude pE sin(theta) is greatest at theta = 90 degrees and zero when p is parallel (or antiparallel) to E.
- Turning the dipole through a small angle -d(theta) takes work dW = tau (-d(theta)), so W = -(integral from theta0 to theta of tau d(theta)) = -pE(-cos(theta)) evaluated from theta0 to theta. Taking theta0 = 90 degrees:

**W = p E cos(theta) = p . E** (Eq. 2.10)

E (Eq. 2.11) but the example then uses the standard form **U = -p . E = -p E cos(theta)**; the minus sign is the standard convention. *(The printed 2.11 looks to have dropped the sign.)*

## pp.67-68 - Misc. Example 4 (where E = 0 for two charges)
- A +10 uC charge is at x = 0 and a -5 uC charge at x = 2 m *(Fig. 2.17)*. Where is E = 0 on the x axis?

  - Between the charges: impossible, because both fields point the same way there (away from +, toward -).

## pp.68-69 - Misc. Example 5 (field on the axis of a charged disc), start
- Find E on the axis of a circular disc of radius a carrying uniform surface charge density **sigma** (C/m^2), at a point P a distance r from its centre.
- Divide the disc into thin rings of radius y and thickness dy *(Fig. 2.18)*. Ring charge: **dq = sigma (2 pi y dy)**. Each point of the ring is at distance r' from P, with r'^2 = y^2 + r^2. Field of the ring element: dE = (1/4 pi eps0) dq / r'^2.
- By symmetry the components perpendicular to the axis cancel, so only the axial part survives: **E = integral of dE cos(theta)**. *(Continues on the next page.)*

## p.70 - Example 5 result (disc axis) and its limits (printed p.81)
- Substituting cos(theta) = r/r' and integrating ring contributions from y = 0 to a (the book uses a table integral from its appendix):

**E = (sigma / 2 eps0) [ 1 - r / sqrt(a^2 + r^2) ]**

- Exercises left to the reader:
  - If a >> r (a very large disc, or the point very close to it), **E = sigma / (2 eps0)**, the field of an infinite sheet, independent of r.
  - If a << r (a small disc seen from far away), E = (1/4 pi eps0) Q/r^2 with Q = pi a^2 sigma, i.e. it looks like a point charge.

## pp.70-72 - Misc. Example 6 (semicircular wire)
- Find E at the centre P of a wire bent into a semicircle of radius a, carrying uniform linear charge density lambda (C/m). *(Fig. 2.19: a half-ring below P, an element dl at angle theta, with dE having components dEx and dEy. theta runs from -pi/2 to +pi/2.)*
- Element field: dE = (1/4 pi eps0) lambda dl / a^2. For every element on one side there is a mirror element on the other, so the horizontal components cancel (Ex = 0) and only Ey survives: E = integral of dE cos(theta).
- With dl = a d(theta): E_y = (lambda / 4 pi eps0 a) times the integral from -pi/2 to pi/2 of cos(theta) d(theta) = (lambda / 4 pi eps0 a)[sin(theta)] from -pi/2 to pi/2, so

**E = lambda / (2 pi eps0 a)**

## pp.72-74 - Misc. Example 7 (finite straight wire)
A straight wire of length L carries uniform charge density lambda. Find E (a) at a point on its axis, (b) at a point on its perpendicular bisector.
- **(a) On the axis** *(Fig. 2.20: wire from x = a to x = L + a, with P at the origin)*: dE = (1/4 pi eps0) lambda dx / x^2. Integrate from a to L + a:

**E = (lambda / 4 pi eps0) [ 1/a - 1/(L + a) ]**, pointing in the -x direction (away from the wire when lambda > 0).

- **(b) On the perpendicular bisector** *(Fig. 2.21: wire from x = -L/2 to +L/2, P at height a above its centre)*: the horizontal components cancel by symmetry, leaving dE cos(theta), with cos(theta) = a / sqrt(x^2 + a^2):

E_y = (lambda a / 4 pi eps0) times the integral from -L/2 to L/2 of dx/(x^2 + a^2)^(3/2) = (lambda a / 4 pi eps0)[ x / (a^2 sqrt(x^2 + a^2)) ] from -L/2 to L/2, so

**E = lambda L / (2 pi eps0 a sqrt(L^2 + 4 a^2))**

(For L >> a this tends to lambda / (2 pi eps0 a), the infinite-wire result of Example 2.5.)

## pp.74-75 - Misc. Example 8 (infinite sheet of charge)
- Find E at distance b from an infinite plane sheet with uniform surface charge density sigma. *(Fig. 2.22: sheet in the xy plane, a strip of width dy running parallel to x, point P on the z axis at height b, with the strip's distance r.)*
- Treat each strip as an infinite wire with line density lambda = sigma dy (Example 2.5): dE = sigma dy / (2 pi eps0 sqrt(b^2 + y^2)).
- The y components cancel in pairs (strips on both sides of the axis), so only the z component remains, with cos(theta) = b / sqrt(b^2 + y^2):

E_z = (sigma b / 2 pi eps0) times the integral from -infinity to +infinity of dy/(b^2 + y^2) = (sigma b / 2 pi eps0)(1/b)[tan^-1(y/b)] from -inf to +inf = (sigma / 2 pi eps0)[pi/2 - (-pi/2)], so

**E = sigma / (2 eps0)**

It is **independent of the distance b** from the sheet. (This matches the large-disc limit in Example 5.)

## pp.76-77 - Misc. Example 9 (charged ball hanging beside an infinite sheet)
- An infinite sheet carries uniform surface density sigma = 4x10^-9 C/cm^2. A ball of mass 1 g with a charge q hangs on a thread and the thread makes **13 degrees** with the sheet. Find q. *(Fig. 2.23: vertical sheet on the left, thread at 13 degrees, forces on the ball: T sin 13 toward the sheet, T cos 13 up, mg down, qE away from the sheet.)*

## pp.77-78 - Misc. Example 10 (electron crossing a uniform field)
- An electron is shot horizontally from A (midway between two horizontal parallel plates) with speed 4x10^6 m/s and leaves at B. The plates are 1.6 cm long and 0.8 cm apart, so the vertical displacement from A to B is 0.4 cm *(Fig. 2.24)*. Find E between the plates (neglect gravity).

# CHAPTER 3: GAUSS'S LAW (PDF pp.89-)

## p.89 - Chapter title page: "Chapter Three - Gauss's Law".
## p.90 - Biography box
Carl Gauss: born 1777, died 1855. Regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians of his age. Made a number of discoveries in physics and astronomy. German nationality. *(Figure: engraved profile portrait.)*

## p.91 - Introduction and 3.1 Electric flux (printed p.103)
- Gauss's law adds almost nothing new beyond Coulomb's law, but it is important because it lets us treat problems with **geometric symmetry** quickly and simply. First the concept of **electric flux** is introduced as the mathematical tool.
- Chapter 2 said the density of field lines is proportional to the field strength. Now the relation is made precise: the number of lines through a unit area, perpendicular to the lines, equals the field **numerically**. The **flux Phi** through an area is the number of field lines passing through it. *(Footnote: area is a vector quantity whose direction is the normal to the surface.)*
- *Fig. 3.1a:* 8 lines cross the area dA perpendicularly (the area vector points along the field). If dA = 1 m^2, the flux is 8 lines, matching a field of 8 N/C.

## p.92 - Flux through tilted areas (printed p.104)
- *Fig. 3.1b:* if the field is parallel to the surface (perpendicular to the area vector) no lines cross it, so **the flux is zero**.
- *Fig. 3.1c:* if the area vector makes angle theta with the field, the lines crossing it are those crossing the projected area A cos(theta). For theta = 60 degrees (as drawn) this gives half of the 8 lines, i.e. 4.

## p.93 - Flux formula and closed surfaces (printed p.105)
- **Phi = E . dA** (3.1), units **N m^2 / C** (field units times area units).
- For a **closed surface** *(Fig. 3.2: an irregular blob in a uniform field)*: every line that enters also leaves. With the convention that inward lines count negative and outward lines positive, the **net flux is zero** when the surface contains no net charge.
- If the closed surface encloses a **positive charge** *(Fig. 3.3a)*, the lines point outward and the net flux is **positive**. If it encloses a **negative charge** *(Fig. 3.3b)*, the lines come in and the net flux is **negative**.

## p.94 - Flux through a closed surface; Example 3.1 (printed p.106)
- *Figs. 3.3a/3.3b:* a closed blob around a positive charge has field lines leaving it; around a negative charge, lines enter it.
- For a closed surface, integrate over the whole surface: **Phi = (closed-surface integral) E . dA** (Eq. 3.2). Split the surface into tiny areas dA, find E . dA for each, then add them up. The circle on the integral sign means a closed surface.
- **Example 3.1:** a square of area 0.3 m^2 sits in a uniform field of 400 N/C. Find the flux when the angle between the field and the area vector is (a) 0, (b) 45, (c) 90, (d) 120, (e) 180 degrees.

## pp.95-96 - Example 3.1 solution (printed pp.107-108)
Phi = E A cos(theta):
- 0 degrees: (400)(0.3)(1.0) = **120 N m^2/C**
- 45 degrees: (400)(0.3)(0.707) = **84.85 N m^2/C**
- 90 degrees: (400)(0.3)(0) = **0**
- 120 degrees: (400)(0.3)(-0.5) = **-60 N m^2/C**
- 180 degrees: (400)(0.3)(-1.0) = **-120 N m^2/C**

Conclusions (Figs. 3.4a to 3.4d):
1. The flux is largest when theta = 0, i.e. the field is perpendicular to the plane of the surface *(Fig. 3.4a)*.
2. The flux is zero when theta = 90 degrees, i.e. the field is parallel to the surface *(Fig. 3.4b)*.
3. The flux is positive when the angle between the field and the area vector is acute *(Fig. 3.4c)*.
4. The flux is negative when the angle is obtuse *(Fig. 3.4d)*.

## pp.97-98 - Example 3.2 (flux through a box in a uniform field) (printed pp.109-110)
- A box (rectangular parallelepiped) of size 0.5 m x 0.5 m x 2 m sits in a uniform field E parallel to its long axis *(Fig. 3.5: field lines enter the left square face and leave the right one; the outward area vector dA is drawn on every face)*. Find the flux through each face and the total.
- Each end face has area 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.25 m^2. On the right end face (field and outward area vector in the same direction): Phi = E A cos(0) = **+0.25 E**. On the left end face the outward area vector points opposite to the field: Phi = E A cos(180) = **-0.25 E**.
- The other four faces are parallel to the field, so E . dA = E dA cos(90) = **0** on each.
- Total: Phi = 0.25 E + (-0.25 E) = **0**. This is expected: a closed surface containing no charge has as many lines entering as leaving, so its net flux is zero.

## p.99 - 3.2 Gauss's law, set-up (printed p.111)
- Start with a **positive point charge Q at the centre of an imaginary sphere** (a "Gaussian surface"; the text calls its size 2R). *(Fig. 3.6: a sphere cut away to show the charge at its centre, with an area element dA and the field E on the surface, both pointing radially outward.)*
- The flux through the sphere is Phi = (closed-surface integral) E . dA. On the sphere's surface the field is **radial**, so at every point it is parallel to dA; and by Eq. 2.2 the field E has the same value everywhere on the sphere, so it can be substituted directly. *(Continues on the next page.)*

## p.100 - Gauss's law for a sphere (printed p.112)
- With E = Q/(4 pi eps0 R^2) constant on the sphere, Phi = (1/4 pi eps0)(Q/R^2) times the closed-surface integral of dA. The integral of dA is the sphere's area 4 pi R^2, so

**Phi = Q_in / eps0** (Eq. 3.3)

- The flux does not depend on the sphere's radius (0.2R, 2R or 10R all give Q/eps0). Equivalently, the number of field lines leaving the charge in all directions is **N = Q / eps0** (Eq. 3.4).
- Generalising: surround the sphere with an irregular closed surface S *(Fig. 3.7)*. Every line crossing the sphere also crosses S.

## p.101 - General form (printed p.113)
- *Fig. 3.7: a positive charge inside an irregular closed surface S with E and dA drawn at three surface points.*

**Phi = N = (closed-surface integral) E . dA = Q_in / eps0** (Eq. 3.5)

- The result depends neither on the shape of the surface nor on where the charge sits inside it; only on the surface containing Q.
- With several charges inside, Q_in is the **net (algebraic) charge**. If the surface in Fig. 3.7 also held a -Q, the net charge would be zero and Phi = 0.

**(closed-surface integral) E . dA = (1/eps0) sum of Q_in** (Eq. 3.6)

## p.102 - Gauss's law stated (printed p.114)
- The sum is over all point charges inside the closed surface, now called the **Gaussian surface**. For a continuous charge distribution, with volume charge density rho and volume V inside the surface:

**(closed-surface integral) E . dA = (1/eps0) integral of rho dV** (Eq. 3.7)

- Eqs. 3.6 and 3.7 are **Gauss's law**. It follows from the inverse-square law (the book cites R. K. Wangsness, *Electromagnetic Fields*, ch. 4, for a proof). It means:
  1. If the algebraic sum of the charge inside the Gaussian surface is zero, the integral is zero.
  2. Charges outside the Gaussian surface do not affect the integral.
  3. The integral depends only on the total charge inside, not on where it is placed.
- **Example 3.3:** find E of a 6 uC point charge at a point 25 cm away, using Gauss's law.

## pp.103-104 - Example 3.3 solution (printed pp.115-116)
- Choose the Gaussian surface (imaginary) so that the field point lies on it and it has enough symmetry to do the integral. Here: a **sphere of radius 25 cm centred on the charge** *(Fig. 3.8: dashed circle with the 6 uC charge at its centre)*.
- On this sphere E is radial (angle with dA is 0) and constant, so E (4 pi r^2) = Q/eps0, giving **E = (1/4 pi eps0) Q/r^2**, the same as Eq. 2.2. (Gauss's law adds nothing new here; its power appears in other applications.)
- Numerically: E = (9x10^9)(6x10^-6)/(0.25)^2 = **8.64x10^5 N/C**.
- **Example 3.4:** find E of an infinite wire with uniform linear charge density lambda, at a distance x from it.

## p.105 - Example 3.4 solution, start (printed p.117)
- Same problem as Example 2.5, but now with Gauss's law, to show how much easier symmetric problems become. Choose as Gaussian surface a **cylinder** coaxial with the wire, length l and radius x *(Fig. 3.9)*.
- Gauss: the integral over the curved side (1) + top (2) + bottom (3) = lambda l / eps0, since the wire section inside carries charge lambda l.
- On the curved side the field is parallel to dA and has the same magnitude everywhere. *(Continues on the next page: on the top and bottom E is parallel to the surface, so those integrals vanish.)*

## p.106 - End of Example 3.4; 3.3 Gauss's law and Coulomb's law (printed p.118)
- On the top and bottom of the cylinder E is perpendicular to dA, so those two integrals are zero. That leaves E (2 pi x l) = lambda l / eps0, where 2 pi x l is the curved area, so

**E = lambda / (2 pi eps0 x)**

This is the same as Example 2.5 with less effort and easier mathematics.
- **3.3 Gauss's law and Coulomb's law:** the force between two point charges q1 and q2 can be found if the field of one at the position of the other is known: F = q2 E1.

## p.107 - Deriving Coulomb's law from Gauss's law (printed p.119)
- To get E1, use a Gaussian sphere of radius r centred on q1, with q2 lying on its surface *(Fig. 3.10: dashed sphere, q1 at the centre, q2 on the surface at distance r)*. The surface encloses only q1.
- Gauss: E1 (4 pi r^2) = q1/eps0, so E1 = (1/4 pi eps0) q1/r^2, and the force on q2 is F = q2 E1.

## p.108 - Coulomb's law recovered; Example 3.5 (printed p.120)
- **F = (1/4 pi eps0) q1 q2 / r^2**, which is Coulomb's law. The magnitude of F does not depend on where q2 sits on the Gaussian surface, though its direction does.
- **Example 3.5:** find the force that an infinite wire with line density 5x10^-9 C/m exerts on a charge of 2.4 uC at a distance 150 cm from it.
- Using Example 2.5: E = lambda / (2 pi eps0 x), F = qE:

F = (2.4x10^-6)(5x10^-9) / [2 (3.14)(8.85x10^-12)(1.5)] = **1.44x10^-4 N**

## pp.109-111 - 3.4 Insulated conductors (printed pp.121-123)
A **conductor** is a material in which charges move freely. Under **electrostatic conditions** the charges have settled into equilibrium, so there is **no current** inside the conductor. Four consequences:

- **(a) E = 0 inside a conductor.** If E were not zero, charges would move under it, producing a current. Also E must be **perpendicular to the surface**; any tangential component would drive charges along the surface.
- **(b) All the charge sits on the outer surface.** Take a Gaussian surface just inside the conductor's surface *(Fig. 3.11)*. Gauss's law gives (closed-surface integral) E . dA = q/eps0. But E = 0 inside, so q = 0: **no net charge inside the conductor**; the charge is on the outer surface only.
- **(c) Induced charge.** A charge Q placed in a cavity inside the conductor body *(Fig. 3.12: shaded conductor, a white cavity with Q in it, and a Gaussian surface drawn within the conductor material)* induces **-Q** on the cavity's inner surface. Proof: E = 0 on the Gaussian surface, so the total enclosed charge is zero: Q + Q_ind = 0, hence **Q_ind = -Q**.
- **Example 3.6:** find the field of a conducting sphere of radius R carrying charge Q at (a) r < R, (b) r = R, (c) r > R. *(Solution on the next page.)*

## pp.112-113 - Example 3.6 solution: charged conducting sphere (printed pp.124-125)
In each case the Gaussian surface is a concentric sphere of radius r *(Fig. 3.13: the shaded conducting sphere of radius R with a dashed spherical Gaussian surface around it)*.
- **r < R:** all the charge is on the outer surface, so nothing is enclosed: E (4 pi r^2) = 0, so **E = 0** (agrees with property (a) of conductors).
- **r = R:** the Gaussian surface is the sphere's own surface: E (4 pi R^2) = Q/eps0, so **E = (1/4 pi eps0) Q/R^2**.
- **r > R:** E (4 pi r^2) = Q/eps0, so **E = (1/4 pi eps0) Q/r^2**.
- Outside (and on the surface of) a charged conducting sphere, the field is that of a point charge Q at the centre.

# CHAPTER 4: ELECTRIC POTENTIAL (الجهد الكهربائي) (PDF pp.132-)

## p.132 - Chapter title page: "Chapter Four - Electric Potential".
## p.133 - Biography box
Alessandro Volta (1745-1827), Italian, inventor of the electric battery (the "voltaic pile"). *(Figure: engraved portrait.)*

## p.134 - Introduction and 4.1 Electric potential (printed p.147)
- Recall from the study of potential energy that a free body always moves from a point of higher potential energy to one of lower potential energy. Gravity gives the familiar example. This chapter develops the electric analogue: **electric potential**.
- A charged body in an electric field feels an electric force. If it is free, it moves from the point of **higher** electric potential toward the point of **lower** potential (for a positive charge). To move a charged body **against** this force, work must be done, just as when you lift a body against gravity.
- **4.1 Electric potential:** the relation between potential difference and field is like the relation between potential energy and gravity. A body gains gravitational potential energy by being lifted; a charge gains electric "potential" by being moved in a field. The **potential difference** between two points A and B is defined as the **work per unit charge needed to move a test charge from A to B**.

## p.135 - Definition of potential difference (printed p.148)

**W(A to B) / q0 = V_B - V_A** (Eq. 4.1)

where q0 is the positive test charge being moved, V_A and V_B are the potentials at A and B, and (V_B - V_A) is the potential difference.
- *Fig. 4.1a:* a uniform field E points right; point B is to the left of A, so moving the charge from A to B is moving it **against** the field.
- *Fig. 4.1b:* a point A and a higher point B above the ground (an analogy to gravity: moving up needs work).
- **Positive work** (done by an external force): B is at a higher potential than A (Fig. 4.1a); we must push the charge. **Negative work** (the field itself does the work): B is at a lower potential than A *(Fig. 4.2a)*, and the charge moves from A to B on its own. **Zero work:** A and B are at the same potential *(Fig. 4.3a)*.

## p.136 - Gravity analogy and the zero of potential (printed p.149)
- *Fig. 4.2a:* the same field, but now B lies in the direction of the field from A. The field itself does the work moving a positive charge from A to B, so the external work is negative and **V_B < V_A**.
- *Fig. 4.2b / 4.3b:* the gravity versions. If B is lower than A, the body falls on its own. If A and B are at the same height, no work is needed (Fig. 4.3b).
- When one of the two points is at **infinity**, its potential is taken as **zero**, just as potential energy at ground level is defined as zero in mechanics. We can then speak of the "electric potential" at a point instead of a potential difference.

## p.137 - Potential at a point; units (printed p.150)

**V = W / q0** (Eq. 4.2)

- Definition: the potential at a point is the work done by an external agent in bringing a unit **positive** charge from infinity (where V = 0) to that point. V is the potential difference between the point and infinity.
- By Eq. 4.1, the work done against the field depends **only on the start and end points, not on the path** taken. This holds only when the forces are **conservative**, as gravity and the electric force are. *(Footnote: see Halliday and Resnick, Physics, Wiley 1978, ch. 29; Marion and Hornyak, Physics, Saunders 1982, ch. 29.)*
- V is a **scalar** (W and q0 are both scalars). The SI unit is the **volt (V)**: **1 V = 1 J/C**.

## p.138 - Uniform field relation (printed p.151)
- A potential difference of 1 volt exists between two points if 1 joule is needed to move a charge of 1 coulomb from one to the other.
- For a charge q0 moved a distance d in a **uniform field** by a force F = q0 E (using Eq. 2.1):

**W(A to B) = q0 E d** (Eq. 4.3), so **V_B - V_A = W/q0 = E d** (Eq. 4.4)

- Equation 4.4 links the field and the potential difference for this simple case, and gives another unit of the electric field: **volt per metre (V/m)**, equal to N/C (the unit from Chapter 2). The book suggests proving that yourself.

## pp.139-141 - Examples 4.1 to 4.4 (printed pp.152-154)
- **Example 4.1:** work to bring +8 C from infinity to a point at +4.5 V. W = q0 V = (8 C)(4.5 V) = **+36 J**. An external force must do this work against the field.
- **Example 4.2:** the same, but the potential at the point is -4.5 V. W = (8)(-4.5) = **-36 J**. This work is done **by the field** when it carries the charge to infinity.
- **Example 4.3:** the work done in bringing 34 C from infinity to a point is 510 J. V = W/q0 = 510/34 = **15 V**.
- **Example 4.4:** work to move an electron between two points with a potential difference of -1.5x10^6 V. W = q0 (V_A - V_B) = (-1.6x10^-19 C)(-1.5x10^6 V) = **2.4x10^-13 J**.
- **Example 4.5:** two parallel plates *(Fig. 4.4)* are charged until the potential difference between them is 36 V, and they are 15 cm apart. Find (a) the field between them, (b) the work to move a charge of -5 uC from the negative plate to the positive plate. *(Solution on the next page.)*

## pp.142-143 - Example 4.5 solution (printed pp.155-156)
- *Fig. 4.4:* two horizontal plates with evenly spaced vertical field lines pointing **downward**, so the top plate is positive (B) and the bottom plate negative (A).
- **(a)** V_B - V_A = E d, so **E = 36 V / 0.15 m = 240 V/m**.
- **(b)** W(A to B) = q0 (V_B - V_A) = (-5x10^-6 C)(36 V) = -180x10^-6 J = **-1.8x10^-4 J**.
- The work is negative, so the charge moves from the negative plate to the positive plate **by itself**, driven by the field force q0 E. This should be clear: the negative charge is repelled from the negative plate and attracted to the positive one.
- Check with Eq. 4.4: W = q0 E d = (-5x10^-6)(240)(0.15) = **-1.8x10^-4 J**.

## p.144 - 4.2 Potential and the electric field (non-uniform field) (printed p.157)
- Eq. 4.4 holds only for a uniform field with the displacement along the field. For a **non-uniform field** with the charge moving along an irregular path *(Fig. 4.5: curved field lines, points A and B, a test charge q0 with a small step dr along a winding path)*, consider the work dW done over a small displacement dr by a force F that is equal and opposite to the electric force, F = -q0 E.

## p.145 - Integral relation between V and E (printed p.158)
- Work-force-distance relation: dW = F . dr. With F = -q0 E (an external force balancing the field force) this gives dW = -q0 E . dr. *(The book prints Eq. 4.5 as dW = q0 E . dr, with the minus sign missing; the next line carries it.)*
- Total work: W(A to B) = -q0 (integral from A to B) E . dr. Substituting into Eq. 4.1:

**V_B - V_A = -(integral from A to B) E . dr** (Eq. 4.6)

- With A at infinity (V = 0), the potential at point B is **V = -(integral from infinity to B) E . dr** (Eq. 4.7).
- Remember: the charge is moved under **balanced forces** (our force against the field force), so it moves at constant speed, with no acceleration.

## pp.146-147 - Example 4.6 (printed pp.159-160)
- A charge q0 is moved without acceleration from A to B along the path A, C, D, B *(Fig. 4.6: a uniform field pointing down. A is at the bottom, B is 6 cm directly above A, the segment AC is 6 cm long and makes 60 degrees with AB, CD is a vertical segment of 3 cm, and DB is horizontal)*. Find V_B - V_A.
- **Path AC:** the angle between the field and the path is 120 degrees. V_C - V_A = -(integral) E cos(120) dr = (1/2) E x (path length 6 cm) = **3x10^-2 E**.
- **Path CD:** the angle is 180 degrees. V_D - V_C = E x (3 cm) = **3x10^-2 E**.
- **Path DB:** D and B are at the same potential (the path is perpendicular to the field, so cos 90 = 0 and the integral is zero).
- Total: V_B - V_A = 3x10^-2 E + 3x10^-2 E + 0 = **6x10^-2 E**. This is also what you get by going straight from A to B (6 cm against the field), since **potential difference is independent of the path**.

## p.148 - 4.3 Potential due to a point charge, set-up (printed p.161)
- The field of a point charge q falls off with distance (Eq. 2.2). Take two points A and B at distances r_A and r_B from q. For simplicity (it is not necessary) choose them on the same radial line *(Fig. 4.7: charge +q with radial field lines; A and B on one line, with a small step dr)*. The field and the step dr point the same way, so the angle between them is zero, and Eq. 4.6 applies: V_B - V_A = -(integral from A to B) E . dr.

## pp.149-150 - Point-charge potential and equipotential surfaces (printed pp.162-163)
- Substituting E from Eq. 2.2: V_B - V_A = -(q / 4 pi eps0)(integral of dr / r^2), i.e.

**V_B - V_A = (q / 4 pi eps0)(1/r_B - 1/r_A)** (Eq. 4.8)

- Taking A at infinity (V_A = 0) the potential at distance r is

**V = (1/4 pi eps0) q / r** (Eq. 4.9)

- So the potential is the same at every point on a sphere of radius r centred on the charge: such a surface is an **equipotential surface**. For a point charge the equipotential surfaces are **concentric spheres**. The potential difference between any two points on one of them is zero, so moving a charge around on it needs **no work**.
- Equivalently, E is always **perpendicular** to an equipotential surface, so in Eq. 4.6 the angle is 90 degrees, cos 90 = 0, the integral vanishes and V_B = V_A.

## pp.151-153 - 4.4 Potential due to a group of point charges (printed pp.164-166)
- Potential is a **scalar**, so the total potential of several point charges is the **algebraic sum** (not a vector sum) of the potentials of each charge separately. *(Fig. 4.8: three charges q1, q2, q3 at distances r1, r2, r3 from the field point P.)*

V = V1 + V2 + V3 = (1/4 pi eps0)(q1/r1 + q2/r2 + q3/r3), and for n charges

**V = sum of V_n = (1/4 pi eps0) times the sum over n of q_n / r_n** (Eq. 4.10)

- For a **continuous** charge (a charged plate, a charged sphere, ...) the sum becomes an integral: **V = integral of dV = (1/4 pi eps0) integral of dq / r** (Eq. 4.11), where dq is a tiny element of charge at distance r from the field point.
- **Example 4.9:** three point charges sit at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side 6 cm *(Fig. 4.9)*: q1 = +8 uC and q2 = -4 uC at the base corners, q3 = -12 uC at the apex. Find V at P, the midpoint of the base (between q1 and q2). *(Solution on the next page.)*

## pp.155-156 - Example 4.10 solution (printed pp.168-169)
- Divide the disc into thin rings of radius y and width dy: **dq = sigma (2 pi y) dy**. Every point of a ring is at the same distance r' = sqrt(y^2 + r^2) from P, so dV = (1/4 pi eps0) dq / r' = (1/4 pi eps0) sigma (2 pi y) dy / sqrt(y^2 + r^2).
- Integrate over all rings from y = b to y = a (table integral from the appendix):

**V = (sigma (2 pi) / 4 pi eps0) [ sqrt(a^2 + r^2) - sqrt(b^2 + r^2) ]**, the potential at any point on the disc's axis.

- **Special case, far away (r >> a and r >> b):** expand sqrt(a^2 + r^2) = r (1 + a^2/r^2)^(1/2), about r + a^2/(2r), and similarly for b. Then V = (sigma pi / 4 pi eps0)(a^2 - b^2)/r = (1/4 pi eps0) q / r, with q = sigma pi (a^2 - b^2) the total charge on the disc. So **far from the disc the potential looks like that of a point charge q at its centre**.

## pp.157-158 - 4.5 Potential due to a dipole (printed pp.170-171)
- An **electric dipole**: two equal and opposite charges separated by 2a, which is very small compared with the distance to the field point P *(Fig. 4.11a: the two charges on a vertical line, a on each side of the midpoint, r1 and r2 the distances to P, r the distance from the midpoint, theta the angle between r and the dipole axis)*.
- The potential at P is the sum of the potentials of the two charges (Eq. 4.10): V = V(+q) + V(-q) = (1/4 pi eps0) q/r1 + (1/4 pi eps0)(-q)/r2, i.e.

**V = (q / 4 pi eps0)(r2 - r1)/(r1 r2)** (Eq. 4.12), where r1 and r2 are the distances of the positive and negative charges from P. This is **exact**.

- If the separation 2a is very small compared with r, then (from the figure) the angle at the negative charge is about theta, so (r2 - r1)/(2a) is about cos(theta), and r1 r2 is about r^2. Substituting:

V is about (q/4 pi eps0)(2a cos(theta))/r^2 = (1/4 pi eps0)(2 a q) cos(theta) / r^2. With the **dipole moment p = 2 a q**:

**V = (1/4 pi eps0) p cos(theta) / r^2** (Eq. 4.13)

## p.159 - Polar molecules and Example 4.11 (printed p.173)
- Many molecules are **polar**: the positive and negative charges are separated by a small distance. A water molecule is, in effect, two electric dipoles (the two O-H bonds) with an angle of **104 degrees** between them *(Fig. 4.12: H at the top, O at the left, H at the bottom, arrows from O toward each H and the 104-degree angle marked)*. When an electric field is applied to the molecules of a material it separates the positive and negative charges and "creates" a dipole, which disappears when the field is removed.

## pp.161-163 - Example 4.12 solution: linear quadrupole (printed pp.175-177)
- *Fig. 4.14:* +q at the top, -2q at the centre, +q at the bottom, with a between neighbours. P is at distance r from the central charge and r1, r2 from the two +q charges. theta is the angle between r and the axis.
- By Eq. 4.10: **V = (q / 4 pi eps0)(1/r)(r/r1 + r/r2 - 2)** (1).
- Express r/r1 and r/r2 using the law of cosines: r1^2 = r^2 + a^2 - 2 a r cos(theta), so r/r1 = [1 + (a^2/r^2 - 2(a/r) cos(theta))]^(-1/2).
- Expand by the binomial series (appendix) (2). Since r >> a, drop every term of order higher than a^2/r^2:
  - (3) **r/r1 = 1 + (a/r) cos(theta) + (a^2/r^2)(3 cos^2(theta) - 1)/2**
  - (4) for r/r2 the same steps with cos(180 - theta) = -cos(theta) give **r/r2 = 1 - (a/r) cos(theta) + (a^2/r^2)(3 cos^2(theta) - 1)/2**
- Substitute (3) and (4) in (1): the first-order terms cancel, leaving **V = (q/4 pi eps0)(1/r)(2a^2/r^2)(3 cos^2(theta) - 1)/2** (5).
- The quantity **Q = 2 a^2 q** is the **quadrupole moment**, so

**V = (1/4 pi eps0)(Q / r^3)(3 cos^2(theta) - 1)/2** (Eq. 6), the potential of a quadrupole at any point at distance r. It falls off as **1/r^3**, compared with 1/r^2 for a dipole and 1/r for a point charge.

## pp.163-165 - 4.6 Electric potential energy (printed pp.177-179)
- From mechanics, the work done equals the change in potential energy: **W(A to B) = U_B - U_A** (Eq. 4.14), for moving a charge q0 from A to B. Also (Eq. 4.1 rewritten) **W(A to B) = q0 V_B - q0 V_A** (Eq. 4.15). Comparing: **U_B = q0 V_B and U_A = q0 V_A**.
- As noted for Eq. 4.1, the work may be positive, negative or zero, depending on whether the energy of q0 at B is greater than, less than, or equal to its energy at A.
- Put A at infinity, where V = 0 and so U = 0: **W = U = q0 V** (Eq. 4.16). The potential energy at a point equals the **work needed to bring the charge from infinity to that point**, which equals the charge times the potential there.
- For a set of point charges q1, ..., qn at distances r1, ..., rn from the point B *(Fig. 4.15)*, V = (1/4 pi eps0) sum of q_n/r_n (Eq. 4.10), so the energy of a charge q0 at B is

**U = (1/4 pi eps0) sum of q0 q_n / r_n** (Eq. 4.17). *(The scan prints the left-hand side of 4.17 as "V = q0 sum V_n"; it is the energy U.)*

- **Example 4.13:** three charges sit at the corners of the right triangle in *Fig. 4.16*: q1 = 2 uC (bottom left), q2 = -5 uC (bottom right, the right angle), q3 = 3 uC (top). The legs are a = 5 cm and the hypotenuse (q1 to q3) is a sqrt(2). Find the mutual potential energy of the charges.
- The mutual energy is the algebraic sum over each pair: **U = U12 + U13 + U23 = (1/4 pi eps0)(q1 q2/a + q1 q3/(a sqrt(2)) + q2 q3/a)**. *(Numerical result on the next page.)*

## p.166 - Example 4.13 result; 4.7 The electron-volt (printed p.180)
- **Example 4.13 result:** U = (9x10^9)[ (2x10^-6)(-5x10^-6)/(5x10^-2) + (2x10^-6)(3x10^-6)/(7.1x10^-2) + (-5x10^-6)(3x10^-6)/(5x10^-2) ] = **-3.7 J**. The negative sign means that **3.7 J of work is needed to pull these charges apart to infinite separation**. If the charges were free, they would move toward each other.
- **4.7 Electron-volt:** the joule is far too large a unit for electrons, atoms and particles, so a small energy unit is used: the **electron-volt (eV)**, the energy an electron gains moving through a potential difference of 1 V. Since the potential energy is q V (Eq. 4.16) and the electron's charge is 1.6x10^-19 C:

**1 eV = (1.6x10^-19 C)(1 V) = 1.6x10^-19 J** (Eq. 4.18)

## p.167 - Larger units; Example 4.14 (printed p.181)
- 1 keV = 10^3 eV, 1 MeV = 10^6 eV, 1 GeV = 10^9 eV.
- **Example 4.14:** electrons in a TV tube are accelerated through 20,000 V from rest. Find their kinetic energy. Work = change in KE: W = (1/2) m v^2 - (1/2) m v0^2 with v0 = 0, and by Eq. 4.16 W = U = qV. So (1/2) m v^2 = (1.6x10^-19)(20,000) = **3.2x10^-15 J = (3.2x10^-15)/(1.6x10^-19) eV = 20 keV**.

## pp.168-169 - 4.8 Calculating E from V (printed pp.182-183)
- Eq. 4.7 gives V from E. Now go the other way. Take a point P(x, y, z) and move it a very small step Delta-x along the x axis to P'(x + Delta-x, y, z) *(Fig. 4.17: axes x, y, z, the two points, with a solid path from the origin to P and a dotted path to P')*. By Eq. 4.6 the potential difference is V(x + Delta-x, y, z) - V(x, y, z) = -(integral from x to x + Delta-x) E_x dx, which for a tiny step is -E_x Delta-x. So

E_x = -[V(x + Delta-x, y, z) - V(x, y, z)] / Delta-x

- As Delta-x goes to zero the right side becomes a **partial derivative**:

**E_x = -dV/dx, E_y = -dV/dy, E_z = -dV/dz** (partial derivatives)

**E(x, y, z) = -(dV/dx i + dV/dy j + dV/dz k)** (Eq. 4.19) *(the scan drops the minus sign in the printed form)*, or **E = -grad V** (Eq. 4.20), where grad V (written with the nabla symbol) is the **gradient** of V. If V is known near a point P, the field there can be calculated from it.
- **Example 4.15:** from V = (1/4 pi eps0) q/r: **E = -dV/dr = (1/4 pi eps0) q / r^2**, the same as Eq. 2.2.

## pp.170-171 - Examples 4.16 and 4.17 (printed pp.184-185)
- **Example 4.16 (field on the axis of a uniformly charged ring):** ring of radius a, total charge q, uniform. *(Fig. 4.18: ring in the xz plane, P on the y axis at height y, an arc element a d(phi).)* First find V at P, then E = -dV/dy.
  - The arc a d(phi) carries dq = lambda a d(phi) (lambda = line density), and r = (y^2 + a^2)^(1/2). So V = (a lambda / 4 pi eps0 (y^2 + a^2)^(1/2)) times the integral of d(phi) from 0 to 2 pi = 2 pi a lambda / (4 pi eps0 (y^2 + a^2)^(1/2)).
  - With q = 2 pi a lambda: **V = (q / 4 pi eps0) 1/(y^2 + a^2)^(1/2)**.
  - **E_y = -dV/dy = (1/4 pi eps0) q y / (y^2 + a^2)^(3/2)**. Also E_x = -dV/dx = 0 and E_z = 0, because V does not depend on x or z. (Compare with Problem 2.31.)

- **Example 10:** an isolated conducting sphere of radius R carries charge q. Find the potential for r > R, r = R and r < R (r measured from the centre).
- Reasoning: the charge spreads over the surface until it settles, and all of it is at the same potential. If two points inside or outside the sphere's surface had different potentials, charge would flow. So in general **the surface of a conductor is an equipotential surface**.

## p.185 - Example 10 solution (printed p.199)
- On an equipotential surface of a conductor, E is **perpendicular** to the surface at every point. Inside the conductor E = 0 (there is no enclosed charge), so the potential inside is **constant**.
- Outside, Gauss's law gives E = (1/4 pi eps0) q/r^2. Using Eq. 4.7: V = -(integral from infinity to r) E . dr = -(q / 4 pi eps0)(integral of dr/r^2) = **(1/4 pi eps0) q / r**.
- This holds for every point outside, and at the surface (r = R): **E = (1/4 pi eps0) q/R^2 and V = (1/4 pi eps0) q/R**.
- Physical picture: bring a positive charge q0 from infinity toward the sphere. The resisting force q0 E grows as we approach and is greatest at the surface, where E and V are greatest. Now imagine a small hole in the sphere and push the charge through it. Inside, E = 0, so no force and **no work** is needed: **the potential inside is the same as the potential on the surface**.

## p.198 - Biography box
Michael Faraday, born in London in 1791, died in 1867. His most important discovery was **electromagnetic induction**, which became the basis for building the electric generator. His idea of the electric field was the starting point from which Maxwell laid the foundations of classical electromagnetic theory. *(Figure: engraved portrait.)*

## p.199 - Introduction and 5.1 Capacitance (printed p.213)
- Electric energy can be stored in a device called a **capacitor (condenser)**. It consists of two metal bodies (conductors) insulated from each other and close together. There are many types; the best known is the **parallel-plate capacitor**.
- The energy stored depends on the **geometric shape** of the capacitor, the **potential difference** between its two plates, and the **type of insulator** that separates them. This chapter studies some types and how these factors affect the key property of capacitors, the **capacitance**.
- **5.1 Capacitance:** from Chapters 3 and 4, the charge on a conductor sits entirely on its surface, and the surface is an equipotential. If we place two insulated conductors carrying equal and opposite charges *(Fig. 5.1)*, the charges on surface A arrange themselves so that A is an equipotential surface, and likewise for surface B. The potential difference between the two surfaces is then the same everywhere.
- Note that A is at the **higher** potential, because work must be done by an external force to move a positive charge from B to A.

## p.200 - The defining relation (printed p.214)
- The total charge on the two surfaces is **zero**, and the magnitude of charge q on each surface is **proportional to the potential difference V** between them *(Fig. 5.1: field lines running from the positively charged conductor on one side to the negative one, bulging outward in a lens shape)*:

q is proportional to V, so **q = C V**, i.e. **C = q / V** (Eq. 5.1)

- C is the constant of proportionality, called the **capacitance of the capacitor**. It depends on the **geometric shape** of the two conductors, the **distance** between them, and the **insulating medium** between them.
- Units: from Eq. 5.1, capacitance has units of charge per potential (C/V). This unit is named the **farad (F)** in honour of Michael Faraday: **1 F = 1 C / 1 V**.

## p.201 - Symbols, Example 5.1 and 5.2 (printed p.215)
- Circuit symbols: two parallel lines for a fixed capacitor; two parallel lines with an arrow through them for a variable capacitor.
- **Example 5.1:** capacitance of a capacitor holding 2 C with 1000 V between its plates: C = q/V = 2 C / 1000 V = **2x10^-3 F**.
- **5.2 Capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor:** two parallel conducting plates of equal area A at a constant separation d. It can be charged by connecting the plates to a dc source (a battery) and then disconnecting: one plate carries +q and the other -q. *(The figure and derivation continue on the next page.)*

## p.202 - Parallel-plate capacitor: field and voltage (printed p.216)
- If the plate separation d is very small compared with the plate dimensions, the field between the plates is **uniform**. *(Fig. 5.2: two horizontal plates, the top one labelled A' (area), separation d, evenly spaced downward field lines, and a dashed Gaussian cylinder of base area A' and height h reaching from inside the top plate into the gap.)*
- Gauss's law on the dashed cylinder: (closed-surface integral) E . dA = q/eps0, so E A' = sigma A' / eps0, i.e. **E = sigma / eps0**. The field between the plates is constant and equals the surface charge density divided by the permittivity constant. *(Compare Misc. Example 9 of Chapter 4.)*
- Since the field is uniform, the potential difference is **V = E d = (sigma / eps0) d**.

## p.203 - Capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor; Example 5.2 (printed p.217)
- C = q/V = sigma A / [(sigma / eps0) d], so

**C = eps0 A / d** (Eq. 5.2)

This formula is specific to the parallel-plate capacitor. The capacitance depends on **geometry only**: the plate area and the separation.
- **Example 5.2:** a capacitor with plate area 1 m^2 and plate separation 2 cm holds a charge of 4.43x10^-7 C at a potential difference of 1000 V. Find the permittivity of free space. From C = q/V = eps0 A/d: eps0 = q d / (V A) = (4.43x10^-7)(2x10^-2) / ((10^3)(1)) = **8.86x10^-12 C^2/(N m^2)**. This is a practical way to measure eps0.

## p.204 - Example 5.3 and practical units (printed p.218)
- **Example 5.3:** the plate area of a 1 F parallel-plate capacitor with a 1 cm gap. A = C d / eps0 = (1)(10^-2) / (8.85x10^-12) = **11.3x10^8 m^2**, a square about **33.6 km** on a side. So the farad is a very large unit, and we use:
  - 1 uF = 10^-6 F
  - 1 nF = 10^-9 F
  - 1 pF = 10^-12 F

## pp.205-206 - 5.3 The cylindrical capacitor (printed pp.219-220)
- Two coaxial metal cylinders of radii a (inner) and b (outer), both of length l, carry charge q (+q on one and -q on the other). Assume the length is much greater than the radius so that edge effects at the ends are negligible. *(The text prints "b >> l", which must be a typo for l >> b.)*
- **Gaussian surface:** a coaxial cylinder of radius r (a < r < b) and length l'. *(Fig. 5.3: cross-section, a dark inner disc, a ring-shaped outer cylinder, and radial field lines between them. The Gaussian surface is a dotted circle between them.)*
- Gauss: E (2 pi r l') = q/eps0 = lambda l / eps0 (lambda is the line density), so **E = (lambda / 2 pi eps0)(1/r)**.
- Potential difference (Eq. 4.6): V = -(integral from a to b) E . dr, which here is (integral from a to b) E dr = (lambda / 2 pi eps0) ln(b/a). With lambda = q/l: **V = (q / 2 pi eps0 l) ln(b/a)**.
- Therefore the capacitance of a cylindrical capacitor is

**C = q/V = 2 pi eps0 l / ln(b/a)**

It again depends only on geometry: the two radii and the length.

## p.207 - Examples 5.4 and 5.5; isolated sphere (printed p.221)
- **Example 5.4:** a cylindrical capacitor has diameters 40 cm and 10 cm (radii 0.2 m and 0.05 m) and length 1.5 m. C = 2 (3.14)(8.85x10^-12)(1.5) / ln(0.2/0.05) = **60 pF**. *(The scan prints the ratio as "2 m / .05 m", which is a typo for 0.2 m / 0.05 m.)*
- **Example 5.5 (capacitance of an isolated sphere):** treat the sphere as a capacitor whose other plate is an infinitely large concentric sphere, where the potential is zero. Then C = q/V with V the potential at the sphere's surface. For a sphere of radius R, solid or hollow, V = (1/4 pi eps0) q/R, so

**C = 4 pi eps0 R**

The capacitance of a spherical capacitor depends only on its radius.

## pp.208-210 - 5.4 Capacitors in parallel and in series (printed pp.222-224)
- **Parallel** *(Fig. 5.4a: C1, C2, C3 stacked between two common rails, joined at points a and b, with the voltage V across the rails)*: every capacitor has the same potential difference V. For each, q = C V, so q1 = C1 V, q2 = C2 V, q3 = C3 V. Adding: q1 + q2 + q3 + ... = (C1 + C2 + C3 + ...) V. With q the total charge on the group and C the equivalent capacitance:

**C = C1 + C2 + C3 + ...** (Eq. 5.3, parallel)

The equivalent capacitance of capacitors in parallel is the **sum** of their capacitances. We can replace the group by a single capacitor C with identical electrical behaviour.
- **Series** *(Fig. 5.4b: four capacitors in a chain with the supply V across the ends)*: the **same charge q** is on every capacitor, while the potential differences V1, V2, V3, ... are different. From V = q/C for each: V1 = q/C1, V2 = q/C2, V3 = q/C3, ... and V = V1 + V2 + V3 + ... = q (1/C1 + 1/C2 + 1/C3 + ...). Dividing by q:

**1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + 1/C3 + ...** (Eq. 5.4, series)

The reciprocal of the equivalent capacitance is the sum of the reciprocals of the individual capacitances.
- **Example 5.6 (parallel):** capacitors C1 = 2 uF, C2 = 1.5 uF, C3 = 4 uF, C4 = 2.5 uF are connected in parallel across 200 V. Find the charge on each. (Solution next.)

## pp.211-212 - Examples 5.6 and 5.7 (printed pp.225-226)
- **Example 5.6 solution:** q = C V on each: **q1 = 400 uC, q2 = 300 uC, q3 = 800 uC, q4 = 500 uC**. Check: the equivalent capacitance is C = 10 uF, so the total charge is (10 uF)(200 V) = 2000 uC, which equals q1 + q2 + q3 + q4. (The book encourages the student to do this check.)
- **Example 5.7 (series):** the same four capacitors are now connected in series across a total of 200 V. Find the voltage across each.
  - 1/C = 1/2 + 1/1.5 + 1/4 + 1/2.5 (in 10^6 F^-1) = (109/60) x 10^6 F^-1, so C = (60/109) uF.
  - The common charge is **q = C V = (60/109 x 10^-6)(200) = 1.1x10^-4 C**.
  - **V1 = q/C1 = 55 V; V2 = q/C2 = 73.33 V; V3 = q/C3 = 27.5 V; V4 = q/C4 = 44 V.** The voltages should add up to the applied 200 V (they do, within rounding).
- **Rule of thumb from these examples:** in parallel the voltage is common and the charges add; in series the charge is common and the voltages add.

## pp.212-213 - 5.5 Energy stored in the electric field (printed pp.226-227)
- Charging a capacitor means moving small positive charges dq from one plate to the other by an external agent such as a battery *(Fig. 5.5: parallel plates with + signs on top and - below, connected to a battery on the right)*, until the potential difference between the plates is V. The work done for each element is **dw = V dq**, and this work is stored as energy in the capacitor.
- If the process continues until the plates carry q, the total work, with V = q/C, is W = (integral from 0 to q) (q/C) dq. This work equals the energy U stored in the capacitor:

**W = U = (1/2) q^2 / C = (1/2) C V^2 = (1/2) q V** (Eq. 5.5)

- The stored energy is proportional to q and to V, and both are proportional to the electric field, so the energy can be regarded as **stored in the electric field**. For a parallel-plate capacitor we know C = eps0 A/d (Eq. 5.2). *(Continues next page.)*

## pp.214-215 - Energy density; Example 5.8 (printed pp.228-229)
- The field between the plates fills the volume A d. Energy per unit volume (**energy density u**): u = U/(A d) = (1/2) C V^2 / (A d). Substituting C = eps0 A/d and E = V/d:

**u = (1/2) eps0 E^2** (Eq. 5.6)

The stored energy density is proportional to the **square of the field strength**.
- **Example 5.8:** a circular parallel-plate capacitor *(Fig. 5.6: a thin disc-shaped capacitor)* is charged until the field between the plates is 8x10^5 V/m. The diameter is 80 cm and the plate separation is 2.5 mm. Find the total stored energy.
  - C = eps0 A/d = (8.85x10^-12)(3.14)(0.4)^2 / (2.5x10^-3) = **1.78x10^-9 F**.
  - V = E d = (8x10^5)(2.5x10^-3) = **2x10^3 V**.
  - U = (1/2) C V^2 = (1/2)(1.78x10^-9)(2x10^3)^2 = **3.56x10^-3 J**.
  - Alternative check: U = (A d) u = A d (1/2) eps0 E^2 gives the same **3.56x10^-3 J**.

## p.216 - 5.6 The dielectric (الدليل / العازل الكهربائي), the experiment (printed p.230)
- So far nothing was said about what lies between the plates, so every formula assumed a vacuum (or air). If an insulator (a **dielectric**) is inserted between the plates, the capacitance **increases**, by an amount that depends on the nature of the dielectric.
- Experiment *(Fig. 5.7: two identical capacitor circuits, each with a voltmeter. In the left one the gap is empty and the reading is V0; in the right one a dark slab of dielectric fills the gap and the reading is V)*: charge a parallel-plate capacitor and connect a **voltmeter** across it. With no dielectric the reading is V0. When the dielectric is inserted the reading **drops** to V. The charge q is the same in both cases, so C0 = q/V0 (vacuum) and C = q/V (with dielectric).

## p.217 - Dielectric constant (printed p.231)
- Since V0 > V, C > C0: the capacitance has increased. The **dielectric constant k** is the ratio of the capacitance with the dielectric to that in vacuum:

**k = C / C0** (Eq. 5.7)

- k is a pure number that carries the electrical and optical properties of the dielectric; Table 5.1 gives it for some materials.
- Vacuum: C0 = eps0 A/d. With the dielectric: **C = eps A/d** (Eq. 5.8), where **eps = k eps0** is the **permittivity of the dielectric**. Note that k has no units, whereas eps has the same units as eps0.
- Inserting a dielectric lets us apply a larger potential difference (hence larger field) across the capacitor. But a dielectric becomes a conductor if the field is too strong, an effect called **dielectric breakdown**. The largest field a dielectric can withstand before breaking down is its **dielectric strength**.

- **Example 5.9:** a parallel-plate capacitor has A = 0.04 m^2 and d = 5 mm. In vacuum the potential difference is V0 = 2.5 kV. When a dielectric is inserted, it drops to 1.5 kV. Find (a) the original capacitance C0, (b) the charge q on each plate, (c) the capacitance C with the dielectric, (d) k, (e) eps, (f) the field E0 in vacuum, (g) the field E with the dielectric.
- *(Footnote: here and in the other examples and problems, when we say a dielectric is between the plates, it is understood to fill the whole space between them unless stated otherwise.)*

## p.219 - Example 5.9 solution, start (printed p.233)
- (a) C0 = eps0 A/d = (8.85x10^-12)(0.04)/(5x10^-3) = **70.8 pF**.
- (b) q = C0 V0 = (70.8x10^-12)(2.5x10^3) = **0.177 uC**.
- (c) C = q/V = (0.177x10^-6)/(1.5x10^3) = **118 pF**.
- (d) k = C/C0 = 118/70.8 = **1.67**. Also k = (q/V)/(q/V0) = V0/V. *(Continues on the next page.)*

## p.220 - Example 5.9 conclusion; Example 5.10 set-up (printed p.234)
- (d) k = V0/V = 2.5x10^3 / 1.5x10^3 = **1.67**. (e) eps = eps0 k = (8.85x10^-12)(1.67) = **14.78x10^-12 C^2/(N m^2)**. (f) E0 = V0/d = 2.5x10^3 / 5x10^-3 = **0.5x10^6 V/m**. (g) E = V/d = 1.5x10^3 / 5x10^-3 = **0.3x10^6 V/m**.
- **Example 5.10:** find the work needed to **remove** a dielectric of k = 2 from between the plates of a capacitor whose capacitance in vacuum is 1.8 pF and whose vacuum potential difference is 1600 V. The work equals the difference between the energy stored with and without the dielectric.

## p.221 - Example 5.10 solution; polar and non-polar dielectrics (printed p.235)
- With the charge q held constant, V = V0/k and C = k C0. W = U0 - U = (1/2) C0 V0^2 - (1/2) C V^2 = (1/2) C0 V0^2 - (1/2)(k C0)(V0/k)^2, so **W = (1/2) C0 V0^2 (1 - 1/k)**. Numerically (1/2)(1.8x10^-12)(1600)^2(1 - 1/2) = **1.15x10^-6 J**. *(The scan prints 7.2x10^-10 J, which is what you get if 1600 is not squared; the printed formula contains V0^2, so the squared value 1.15x10^-6 J is the consistent answer.)*
- Remark: the energy stored with the dielectric is **less** than in vacuum; the "missing" energy shows up as the work that pulls the dielectric into the capacitor.
- **How does a dielectric reduce the potential difference and the field?** First classify dielectrics:
  - **Polar:** the molecules have a **permanent dipole moment**, but without an applied field they point in random directions.
  - **Non-polar:** the molecules have no permanent dipole moment, but in an electric field the positive and negative ions are pushed in opposite directions and form a dipole; the molecules are then said to be **polarized**.
- When a dielectric (either kind) is placed in a field, the electric force turns or creates dipoles lined up with the field, and the stronger the field the better the alignment.

## p.222 - Induced charges; Example 5.11 set-up (printed p.236)
- The net effect of the alignment is a **positive induced charge** on the dielectric surface facing the negative plate, and a **negative induced charge** on the surface facing the positive plate. These induced charges *(Fig. 5.8: a dark slab between two plates, original field E0 pointing down, the induced field E' pointing opposite, and the net E = E0 - E')* set up a field **E' opposite to the original field E0**, so the resultant is E = E0 - E', **weaker than E0**, i.e. V < V0. The induced positive and negative charges must be equal, because the dielectric as a whole stays neutral.
- **Example 5.11:** the space between the plates of a capacitor of area 80 cm^2 and charge 1.6x10^-8 C is filled with a dielectric. The field due to the dielectric's induced charge is 5.8x10^4 V/m (the problem text says "the field inside the dielectric", but the solution uses it as E'). Find the field between the plates.

## p.223 - Example 5.11 solution; 5.7 Dielectric and Gauss's law (printed p.237)
- Want E = E0 - E'. E0 = sigma/eps0 = q/(A eps0) = (1.6x10^-8)/[(80x10^-4)(8.85x10^-12)] = **22.6x10^4 V/m**. So **E = 22.6x10^4 - 5.8x10^4 = 16.8x10^4 V/m**.
- **5.7 Dielectric and Gauss's law:** take a parallel-plate capacitor with plate area A and charge q, with a vacuum between the plates *(Fig. 5.9)*. A Gaussian surface enclosing only the +q plate charge gives, as before, (next page) E0 = q/(eps0 A).

## pp.224-225 - Gauss's law inside a dielectric (printed pp.238-239)
- *Fig. 5.9:* a dotted Gaussian surface hugging the top plate (+q), the field E0 pointing down to the -q plate. In vacuum: (closed-surface integral) E0 . dA = q/eps0, so **E0 = q/(eps0 A)**.
- *Fig. 5.10 (dielectric slab between the plates, a dotted Gaussian surface enclosing the top plate and the slab's top face):* the surface now encloses two charges: the **free charge +q** that was originally on the plate and the **induced charge -q'** on the surface of the dielectric. Gauss's law gives (closed-surface integral) E . dA = (q - q')/eps0, so

**E = q/(eps0 A) - q'/(eps0 A)** (Eq. 5.9)

- But k = C/C0 = V0/V = E0/E, so **E = E0/k = q / (k eps0 A)** (Eq. 5.10). Substituting into 5.9: q/(k eps0 A) = q/(eps0 A) - q'/(eps0 A) (Eq. 5.11), i.e.

**q' = q (1 - 1/k)** (Eq. 5.12)

- So the induced charge is always **smaller than q**, as expected, and **q' = 0 when k = 1** (no dielectric, or the "dielectric" is vacuum).
- Writing Gauss's law again in the presence of the dielectric (Eq. 5.13): (closed-surface integral) E . dA = (q - q')/eps0; substituting q' from Eq. 5.12 gives the compact form (next page).

## p.226 - Gauss's law in a dielectric; Example 5.12 set-up (printed p.240)
- Substituting q' from Eq. 5.12 into Eq. 5.13: (closed-surface integral) E . dA = q/(k eps0). Since eps = k eps0:

**(closed-surface integral) E . dA = q / eps** (Eq. 5.14)

This is **Gauss's law in the presence of a dielectric**. It was derived for a parallel-plate capacitor but holds in general. Eqs. 5.13 and 5.14 are exactly equivalent.
- **Example 5.12:** a dielectric with k = 5 is inserted between the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor of plate area 120 cm^2 and spacing d = 28 mm *(Fig. 5.11: the dotted Gaussian surface around the top plate, with the +q and -q' charge marks on the slab surface)*. The potential difference before inserting it is 75 V. Compute (a) the capacitance before insertion, (b) the free charge q, (c) the field in vacuum, (d) the field in the dielectric.

## p.227 - Example 5.12 solution (printed p.241)
- (a) C0 = eps0 A/d = (8.85x10^-12)(120x10^-4)/(28x10^-3) = **3.79 pF**.
- (b) q = C0 V0 = (3.79x10^-12)(75) = **2.84x10^-10 C**.
- (c) E0 = q/(eps0 A) = (2.84x10^-10)/[(8.85x10^-12)(120x10^-4)] = **2.68x10^3 V/m**. Check: E0 = V0/d = 75 / 28x10^-3 = 2.68x10^3 V/m.
- (d) By Gauss's law in the dielectric, E = q/(eps A) = q/(k eps0 A) = E0/k = (2.68x10^3)/5 = **0.54x10^3 V/m**. The same result follows from a Gaussian surface (as in Fig. 5.11) enclosing the free charge q and the induced charge q' and using Eq. 5.13.

## p.228 - 5.8 The electric vectors D, E and P: polarization (printed p.242)
- Rewrite Eq. 5.11 as **q/A = eps0 (q / (k eps0 A)) + q'/A** (Eq. 5.15). The last term is the **surface density of the induced charge**, which arises from the polarization of the dielectric in the field. It is also written

**P = q'/A = q' d / (A d)** (Eq. 5.16)

where q' d is the **dipole moment** and A d is the **volume** of the dielectric between the plates. P is called the **electric polarization**: the **dipole moment per unit volume**. It is a vector, pointing like the dipole moment, from the induced negative charge toward the induced positive charge *(Fig. 5.12)*.
- The quantity q/(k eps0 A) in Eq. 5.15 is the field E in the dielectric (Eq. 5.10), so **q/A = eps0 E + P**.

## p.229 - Electric displacement D (printed p.243)

**D = eps0 E + P** (Eq. 5.17)

- **D** is the **electric displacement**, also a vector. D and P have the same units, **C/m^2**.
- *Fig. 5.12:* the left plate (+) and right plate (-) with a dark dielectric slab in between. Solid lines (D) run from one plate to the other unbroken, through both the gap and the slab. Dotted lines (P) exist only inside the slab. Dash-dot lines (E) run in the gaps and through the slab, drawn thinner (fewer) inside the slab.

## p.230 - Gauss's law in terms of D; what each vector depends on (printed p.244)

**(closed-surface integral) D . dA = q** (Eq. 5.20)

This is Gauss's law "in another form": the flux of D depends only on the **free** charge enclosed.
- **P** depends only on the **polarized (induced) charges**. Its direction runs from the induced negative charges to the induced positive charges.
- **D** depends only on the **free charges**. Its direction runs from the free positive charges to the free negative charges.
- **E** depends on **all** the charges (free or induced). Its direction runs from positive charges to negative ones, whether the charges are free or induced.

# CHAPTER 6: CURRENT ELECTRICITY (الكهرباء التيارية) (PDF pp.254-)

## p.254 - Chapter title page: "Chapter Six - Current Electricity".
## p.255 - Biography box
George Ohm (1787-1854), German; formulated the law that bears his name. *(Figure: engraved portrait.)*

## p.256 - Introduction and 6.1 Current and current density (printed p.273)
- All earlier chapters dealt with charges at rest (**electrostatics**). When charges **move**, an electric current results, with richer phenomena and a new branch of science, **electrodynamics**. This chapter concentrates on a special case: **steady currents** in one direction, called **direct current (DC)**, caused by electrons moving in conducting materials.
- For such a current to persist, some force must keep doing work to push the charges round the circuit again and again. That force is the **electromotive force (emf)**.
- **6.1 Current and current density:** in a conductor such as copper, the free electrons move randomly and not bound to any particular nucleus, so there is no net current. If we take a wire of conducting material and connect its ends to a battery, the electrons feel a force from the potential difference and drift **against** the field set up in the wire: an electric current now flows through the wire.

## p.257 - Definition of current (printed p.274)
- By convention the **direction of the current is opposite to the direction in which the electrons move**, i.e. the direction in which positive charges would move, the direction of the electric field. The **magnitude** is the net charge crossing a given area A per unit time. If charge Delta-q passes in time Delta-t:

**I_ave = Delta-q / Delta-t** (Eq. 6.1)

- If the rate of flow varies with time, the instantaneous current is

**I = dq/dt** (Eq. 6.2)

- Current is a **scalar**; the "direction" is just the sense of flow along the wire. Its SI unit is the **ampere (A)**, one of the base units, like the metre and kilogram. *(Footnote: the ampere is the current that, flowing in each of two long parallel wires 1 m apart in vacuum, makes each exert a force of 2x10^-7 N per unit length on the other.)* The current in a wire does not change from one point to another even if the cross-section changes.

## p.258 - Current in terms of drift velocity (printed p.275)
- Another way to express I: consider an area A of the wire *(Fig. 6.1: a cylindrical conductor of cross-section A; the arrow v dt shows the length swept in time dt; E and I point right, the electron e's drift arrow points left)*. Electrons pass through it in a short time dt with an average **drift velocity v_d**. The charge that passes in that time is **dq = n e A v_d dt**, where n is the number of free electrons per unit volume and e is the electron charge. Substituting into Eq. 6.2:

**I = dq/dt = n e A v_d** (Eq. 6.3)

- I seems to depend on A, but if A changes I stays constant, because v_d changes so that **A v_d is constant**. This is the same idea as the **equation of continuity** in fluids. *(Footnote: see Rahil and Abu Ain, University Physics, p.168.)*

## p.259 - Current density; Example 6.1 set-up (printed p.276)
- The **current density J** is the current per unit area: **J = I / A** (Eq. 6.4). Substituting Eq. 6.3: **|J| = n e |v_d|** (Eq. 6.5).
- The unit of J is **A/m^2**. By Eq. 6.5, J is a **vector** quantity, because v_d is a vector.
- **Example 6.1:** a current of 3 A flows in a copper wire of cross-section 6 mm^2. Find (a) the number of free electrons per cm^3, (b) the charge that crosses any area in 10 minutes, (c) the current density, (d) the drift velocity, (e) the time for electrons to move 1 cm.

## pp.260-261 - Example 6.1 solution (printed pp.277-278)
- **(a)** There is one free electron per atom, so n equals the number of atoms per unit volume: **n = rho N_A / A_m**, where rho = 8.93 g/cm^3 is the density of copper, N_A = 6.022x10^23 /mol is Avogadro's number, and A_m = 63.5 amu is the atomic mass of copper. n = (8.93)(6.022x10^23)/63.5 = **8.5x10^22 electrons/cm^3**.
- **(b)** Delta-q = I Delta-t = (3 A)(10 x 60 s) = **1.8x10^3 C**.
- **(c)** J = I/A = (3 A)/(6x10^-6 m^2) = **5x10^5 A/m^2**.
- **(d)** From |J| = n e |v_d|: 5x10^5 = (8.5x10^22 electrons / 10^-6 m^3)(1.6x10^-19 C) v_d, so **v_d = 3.7x10^-5 m/s**.
- **(e)** s = v_d t: 1x10^-2 m = (3.7x10^-5 m/s) t, so **t = 270 s, about 4.5 minutes**. (Electrons drift very slowly even though the current appears instantly.)

## p.261 - 6.2 Resistance and resistivity, start (printed p.278)
If a wire is connected to a source and the potential difference across its ends is V while the current is I, the ratio V/I is **constant** even if V and I change. A second wire of another material, with the same length and cross-section, also gives a constant ratio V/I, but with a different value. *(Continues on the next page.)*

## p.262 - Resistance and resistivity (printed p.279)
- The ratio V/I defines the **resistance R** of the wire: **R = V/I** (Eq. 6.6). The SI unit is the **ohm (symbol: omega)**: **1 ohm = 1 V / 1 A**.
- If the wire's geometry changes (length or cross-section), R changes. But a physical quantity that depends **only on the material**, not on the shape, can be defined: the **resistivity rho**. Experiments show that the current density J is proportional to the field E, and the resistivity is the ratio:

**rho = |E| / |J|** (Eq. 6.7)

- Since E and J can be expressed through the measurable V and I (with E = V/l and J = I/A): rho = (V/l)/(I/A).

## p.263 - R = rho l / A and Ohm's law (printed p.280)
- l is the length of the wire and A its cross-section, so **V/I = rho l/A** (Eq. 6.8). Comparing with Eq. 6.6:

**R = rho l / A** (Eq. 6.9)

The resistance of a uniform wire is **proportional to its length**, **inversely proportional to its cross-section**, and proportional to the **resistivity** of the material. Resistivity has units of **ohm metre** (Table 6.1 gives values for some materials).
- Eq. 6.8 can be written **V = I R** (Eq. 6.10), which is called **Ohm's law**.

## p.265 - Temperature dependence; Example 6.2 set-up (printed p.282)
- Equivalently **|J| = (1/rho) |E|** (Eq. 6.11b).
- Experiment shows resistivity depends on **temperature**: for metals it increases linearly with temperature:

**rho_t = rho_0 [1 + alpha (T - T0)]** (Eq. 6.12a)

where rho_t is the resistivity at temperature T, rho_0 that at T0, and **alpha** is the **temperature coefficient of resistivity** (units per degree C; Table 6.1 lists values). Note that alpha is **negative for semiconductors** (carbon, germanium): their resistivity falls as they warm.
- **Example 6.2:** an aluminium wire 5.7 m long with cross-section 3 mm^2 carries 10 A. Find the field in the wire, the potential difference across its ends, and its total resistance. Start from Eq. 6.7 written as **E = rho J**.

## p.266 - Example 6.2 solution; Example 6.3 set-up (printed p.283)

- Potential difference (Eq. 4.4, with d the length of the wire): **V = E d = (8.77x10^-2)(5.7) = 0.5 V**.
- Resistance two ways: R = V/I = 0.5/10 = **0.05 ohm**, or R = rho l/A = (2.63x10^-8)(5.7)/(3x10^-6) = **0.05 ohm**.
- **Example 6.3:** find the resistance of a silver wire 20 m long and 1 mm in diameter at 20 degrees C, and its resistance if the temperature rises to 280 degrees C.

## p.267 - Example 6.3 solution (printed p.284)
- At 20 C, rho (silver) = 1.47x10^-8 ohm m: R = (1.47x10^-8)(20)/(3.14 x 0.5^2 x 10^-6) = **0.37 ohm**.
- Since resistance is proportional to resistivity, and neglecting the wire's thermal expansion, Eq. 6.12a can be written for resistance: **R_T = R_0 [1 + alpha (T - T0)]** (printed as Eq. "7.12b"; it should be 6.12b). With alpha (silver) = 0.0038 per degree C:
R_T = 0.37 [1 + 0.0038 (280 - 20)] = **0.74 ohm**.

## p.268 - 6.3 Ohm's law (printed p.285)
- Ohm's law (Eq. 6.10) is **not a fundamental law of physics**: it depends on the properties of the material carrying the current, and many materials do not obey it. It is nevertheless worth studying because it is useful in most ordinary conditions of voltage and temperature.
- **Experiment** *(Fig. 6.2: a battery with a rheostat and ammeter A in series with the sample R1, and a voltmeter V across the sample)*: connect a metal sample of resistance R1 in a circuit, measure the current with an ammeter and the potential difference across the sample with a voltmeter, and vary the current with a **rheostat** (variable resistor). Record I and V at each setting and plot them *(Fig. 6.3: V on the vertical axis, I on the horizontal; two straight lines through the origin, the steeper labelled R2 and the other R1)*. The graph is a **straight line**, and the ratio V/I gives the constant **R1**, the resistance of the sample.

## p.269 - Ohmic and non-ohmic resistances; Example 6.4 set-up (printed p.286)
- A sample of a different material (and the same geometry) also gives a straight line, but with a different slope, which represents its resistance **R2**.
- A sample made of a **semiconductor** gives a relation between V and I that is **not linear** *(Fig. 6.4: I against V, a curve that stays nearly flat and then rises steeply)*. Resistances that obey Ohm's law are called **ohmic (linear) resistances**.
- **Example 6.4:** the following readings were taken across a resistor: V = 0, 3.7, 7.2, 12.3, 16.3 volts with I = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 amperes. Plot the relation and find R.

## p.270 - Example 6.4 solution; 6.4 Energy and power (printed p.287)
- *(Graph of V in volts against I in amperes, a straight line from the origin.)* The slope of the line is **2.1 ohm**, which is R.
- **6.4 Energy and power in electric circuits:** consider the circuit in *Fig. 6.5* (a battery driving a current I through a box AB, with + at A and - at B). The box may be a resistor, a motor or any electrical device. Suppose a charge Delta-q moves from A to B in a time Delta-t because of the potential difference V_AB between them.

## pp.271-273 - Power (printed pp.288-289; the scan repeats printed p.288, PDF p.271 and p.272)
- *(Note: PDF p.271 and p.272 are two copies of printed p.288; the second has pencil marks. From PDF p.273, printed page = PDF page + 16.)*
- By Eq. 4.16 the change in the charge's potential energy is **Delta-U = Delta-q V_AB**, and since Delta-q = I Delta-t (Eq. 6.1), **Delta-U = I Delta-t V_AB**.
- A is at higher potential than B, so the charge **loses** energy that is gained by the device. The rate of energy loss is the **power**:

**P = Delta-U / Delta-t = I V_AB** (Eq. 6.13)

where P is the electrical power delivered between A and B. Its units are **V A = J/s**, i.e. the **watt (W)**.
- What kind of energy the device gains depends on the device: a **motor** produces mechanical energy; a **battery being charged** stores chemical energy; a **resistor** between A and B turns it into **heat**. In the resistor case, apply Ohm's law (V = I R) to write the power in other forms:

**P = I^2 R** (Eq. 6.14) and **P = V^2 / R** (Eq. 6.15)

These are called **Joule's law**, the conservation of energy in a special case: electrical energy converting to heat.
- **Example 6.5:** an air conditioner operates at 220 V and consumes 1600 W. Find the current and the resistance. From P = I V: 1600 = I (220), so **I = 7.27 A**. From P = V^2/R: **R = (220)^2 / 1600 = 30.25 ohm**.

## p.274 - Example 6.6; 6.5 Resistors in series and parallel, intro (printed p.290)
- **Example 6.6:** a motor needs 1.5 hp when a current of 5 A flows through it. Find the potential difference across it (1 hp = 746 W, "horse power"). From P = I V: (1.5 x 746) = (5) V, so **V = 223.8 V**.
- **6.5 Resistors in series and in parallel:** most circuits have more than one resistor, connected in more than one way. If the same current flows through all of them in turn (*Fig. 6.6*), they are **in series**. If the current divides among the branches, but the **potential difference across them is the same** (*Fig. 6.7*), they are **in parallel**. In each case we look for the **equivalent resistance**: the single resistance that could replace the group without changing the current.

## pp.275-276 - Series and parallel formulas (printed pp.291-292)
- *Fig. 6.6 (series): R1, R2, R3 in a row between A and the battery, with voltages V1, V2, V3 across them, current I. Fig. 6.7 (parallel): R1, R2, R3 as three branches between A and B.*
- **Series:** the voltage between A and B is the sum of the voltages across each resistor, V = V1 + V2 + V3. The same I flows through each, so by Ohm's law V = I R1 + I R2 + I R3, i.e. V/I = R1 + R2 + R3. Since V/I is the total resistance between A and B:

**R = R1 + R2 + R3** (Eq. 6.16)

In series the equivalent resistance is the **sum** of the resistances, however many there are.
- **Parallel:** the current splits at A into I1, I2, I3 through R1, R2, R3. Charge conservation requires the total current entering at A to equal the sum of the branch currents: I = I1 + I2 + I3. With the same V across each resistor, Ohm's law gives V/R = V/R1 + V/R2 + V/R3, so

**1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3** (Eq. 6.17)

The reciprocal of the equivalent resistance is the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances.

## p.277 - Example 6.7 (printed p.293)
*(Fig. 6.8: 7 ohm in series with a parallel pair of 12 ohm and 18 ohm, then in series with 9 ohm.)* The 12 and 18 ohm resistors are in parallel: 1/R = 1/12 + 1/18, so R = 7.2 ohm. This is in series with the 7 ohm and 9 ohm resistors, so the equivalent resistance is R = 7 + 7.2 + 9 = **23.2 ohm**.

## p.278 - 6.6 Electromotive force (printed p.294)
- Current is charge in motion, and for charge to keep moving it needs an external source of energy. There are many sources: **batteries and cells, generators, solar cells.** Batteries and cells convert **chemical** energy into electrical energy; a generator converts **mechanical** energy into electrical energy; one of the newest hopes, which humanity wants to exploit in abundance and efficiently, is converting **solar** energy into electrical energy through solar cells.
- All sources of electrical energy are called sources of **electromotive force (emf)**, symbol **epsilon**. The book stresses that it is **not a force**, but energy per charge; the name is simply widespread. The emf is **the work done to move 1 C of charge through the source**: **epsilon = W/q**, units J/C or V. *(Fig. 6.9: a rectangular circuit with a resistor R on top, and a battery with internal resistance r at the bottom; terminals P+ and P-; the current I flows up one side and down the other. Caption: the motion of a charge in an electric circuit.)*

## p.279 - The circuit equation (printed p.295)
- A charge of 1 C moving round the circuit of Fig. 6.9 from P+ to P- through the circuit gives up a certain energy; moving from P- to P+ through the source it gains exactly the same amount. So the charge returns to its starting point with the same energy it began with, and the process repeats. The emf of the source equals the **change in potential energy** per coulomb: **epsilon = Delta-V**. epsilon is a **rise in potential**; Delta-V is a **drop in potential**.
- When the current I flows in the circuit, it meets the resistor R, and also resistance inside the source itself, because all real sources have an **internal resistance r**. The rise in potential must equal the total drop:

**epsilon = I r + I R**, i.e. **I = epsilon / (r + R)** (Eq. 6.18)

## p.280 - Meaning of Eq. 6.18; Example 6.8 set-up (printed p.296)
- Eq. 6.18 says that **the current in a circuit equals the emf divided by the sum of all the resistances, internal and external**.
- **Example 6.8:** a current of 4 A flows in the circuit of *Fig. 6.10* (an ammeter A and the resistor R in series with a source of internal resistance r, and a voltmeter V connected across the source terminals a and b). R = 20 ohm and the internal resistance is 1.5 ohm. Find the source's emf and the voltmeter reading.
- The emf is the rise in potential, so from Eq. 6.18: **epsilon = I (r + R) = (4 A)(1.5 ohm + 20 ohm) = 86 V**.

## p.281 - Example 6.8 conclusion; Example 6.9 set-up (printed p.297)
- The drop across the internal resistance is V = I r = (4)(1.5) = **6 V**. The voltmeter reading is the difference between the rise and this drop: **86 V - 6 V = 80 V** (the "terminal voltage").
- Note that **with the circuit open (no current), the voltmeter reads 86 V, i.e. the emf**. The student should also verify that the potential drops across internal and external resistances add up to the emf: epsilon = I r + I R.
- **Example 6.9:** in *Fig. 6.11*, the source has emf 18 V and internal resistance 2 ohm; the external circuit is a **parallel pair 16 ohm and 4 ohm** in series with an ammeter. Find the ammeter reading.

## p.282 - Example 6.9 solution (printed p.298)
- To find the current (the ammeter reading) first find the total resistance. 16 ohm and 4 ohm are in parallel: 1/R = 1/16 + 1/4, so **R = 3.2 ohm**. With the internal resistance, the total is R + r = 3.2 + 2 = **5.2 ohm**.
- From Eq. 6.18: **I = epsilon / (r + R) = 18 V / 5.2 ohm = 3.46 A**. The ammeter reads 3.46 A.

## p.283 - 6.7 Kirchhoff's laws, introduction (printed p.299)
- In the previous examples the current could be found from the total resistance and Ohm's law, which works for many circuits. But often the current **cannot** be found that way. In the circuit of *Fig. 6.12* (a two-battery circuit) the total resistance cannot be found, so we must use Kirchhoff's laws.
- *Fig. 6.12: a rectangular network with corners a, b, c, d, e, f. Top branch b-c: 30 ohm. Middle branch from d to a: emf epsilon_2 = 45 V in series with 38 ohm (and another resistor drawn in the same branch). Bottom branch e-f: epsilon_1 = 80 V in series with 6 ohm. Left branch a-f: 15 ohm. Currents I1 (top loop), I2 (bottom/left), I3 (middle branch) are marked; loop I is the upper circulation arrow and loop II the lower.*
- Two definitions first: a **branch point (junction)** is a point where **three or more** conductors meet, like a and d. A **loop** is any closed path in the circuit, like abcda and adefa.

## p.284 - The two laws (printed p.300)
Kirchhoff's laws are applications of two fundamental laws of physics: **conservation of charge** and **conservation of energy**.
- **First law (junction rule):** the sum of the currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving it; the algebraic sum of the currents at any junction is zero:

**sum of I = 0** (Eq. 6.19)

- **Second law (loop rule):** around any closed loop, the algebraic sum of the emfs equals the algebraic sum of the voltage drops IR:

**sum of epsilon = sum of I R** (Eq. 6.20)

- The first law says that the charge entering a point in a given time equals the charge leaving it: at the junctions a and b of Fig. 6.12, **I3 = I1 + I2**. The second law says that the energy supplied by the sources equals the energy lost by the currents as they flow through the resistors.
- Rules for applying them: **(1)** in every loop you choose, assume the direction you want to travel. In Fig. 6.12, loop I is clockwise and loop II is counterclockwise; you may choose either direction.

## p.285 - Rules continued; Example 6.10 set-up (printed p.301)
- **(2)** Add the currents at each junction algebraically; the sum is zero.
- **(3)** Travel round the loop in the chosen direction and add algebraically the emfs and the potential differences I R across each resistor. An **emf is positive** when we pass through the source from its **minus to its plus** terminal. For **I R**: the book counts it **negative** if the current passes through the resistor in the direction we assumed for travelling, and **positive** if the current flows opposite to that direction. The algebraic sum of the emfs and I R terms is zero.
- **Example 6.10:** find the currents I, I1 and I2 in the circuit of *Fig. 6.13* (a 40 V battery on the top branch, a 12 ohm resistor on the right-hand side carrying I, a 10 ohm resistor between a and b carrying I1, and a 40 ohm resistor on the bottom carrying I2). Although the currents could be found without Kirchhoff's laws, we use them to illustrate. At the junction a: **I = I1 + I2** (a).

## p.286 - Example 6.10 solution; Example 6.11 set-up (printed p.302)
- Three unknowns need three equations; (a) is one. **Loop I** (taken counterclockwise): **+40 - 10 I1 - 12 I = 0**, i.e. **40 = 10 I1 + 12 I** (b). **Loop II** (also counterclockwise): **-40 I2 + 10 I1 = 0**, so **I1 = 4 I2** (c).
- Substituting I from (a) into (b): **40 = 22 I1 + 12 I2** (d). Then (c) in (d): 40 = 88 I2 + 12 I2 = 100 I2, giving **I2 = 0.4 A, I1 = 1.6 A, and I = 2 A**. This is what the equivalent-resistance method would also give (R = 12 + (10 x 40)/(10 + 40) = 20 ohm, so I = 40/20 = 2 A).
- **Example 6.11:** in the circuit of Fig. 6.12, find I1, I2 and I3.

## pp.287-288 - Example 6.11 solution (printed pp.303-304)
- Junction a: **I3 = I1 + I2** (a). Three unknowns, so two loop equations are needed.
- **Loop I (clockwise):** +45 - 3 I3 - 38 I3 - 30 I1 = 0, so **45 = 41 I3 + 30 I1** (b). *(The middle branch holds the 45 V source with a 3 ohm internal resistance in series with the 38 ohm resistor, so its total resistance is 41 ohm.)*

## pp.288-290 - 6.8 Electrical measuring instruments (printed pp.304-306)
- **(a) Galvanometer:** one of the basic instruments of electrical measurement. It consists of a **coil** free to rotate about an axis, in a **magnetic field**, with a pointer that moves over a scale *(Fig. 6.14: a cut-away view with a dark central rotor, radial field arrows, and a pointer sweeping a curved scale at the top)*. When a current passes through the coil it produces its own magnetic field, which interacts with the original field and makes the coil rotate. The deflection is **proportional to the current**.
- **(b) Ammeter:** a galvanometer with a **very small resistance R, called a shunt, connected in parallel** with it *(Fig. 6.15: current I enters; G sits in a loop above the resistor R, in parallel with it)*. The ammeter is connected **in series** in the circuit and measures the current.
- **(c) Voltmeter:** measures the potential difference between two points of a circuit, and is connected **in parallel** across the two points. It is a galvanometer connected **in series with a large resistance R** *(Fig. 6.16: current I enters through R and then G)*, so that the current through the voltmeter is very small and does not disturb the current in the circuit.

## pp.290-291 - 6.9 RC circuits (printed pp.306-307)
- In all the circuits so far, emfs and resistances were constant, so potential differences and currents were constant. If the circuit contains a **capacitor**, as in *Fig. 6.17* (a battery, a two-way switch S with contacts a and b, a capacitor C, a resistor R and an ammeter A; caption: charging and discharging circuit of a capacitor), the current **changes with time**.
- **Charging (S at a):** assume the capacitor is initially uncharged. At t = 0 the potential difference across C is zero, so the whole emf appears across R: **epsilon = I R at t = 0**, i.e. **I = epsilon / R at t = 0**.
- Once charge begins to accumulate on the plates, the potential difference across the capacitor grows with time and that across R shrinks, so the **current decreases**. When the capacitor is fully charged the voltage across R is zero, **I = 0**, and the voltage across C equals the emf.
- **Energy balance** over a short time dt: the emf does work **epsilon dq** (dq is the charge that passes in dt). This must equal the heat lost in R, **I^2 R dt**, plus the energy added to the capacitor, **d(q^2/2C)** (C the capacitance):

**epsilon dq = I^2 R dt + d(q^2 / 2C)**, or **epsilon dq = I^2 R dt + (q/C) dq**

## p.292 - Charging an RC circuit (printed p.308)
- Dividing the energy equation by dt and using I = dq/dt (Eq. 6.2):

**epsilon = I R + q/C** (Eq. 6.21a). (The same equation follows from Kirchhoff's second law.) Written with I = dq/dt: **epsilon = R dq/dt + q/C** (Eq. 6.21b).

- Solving this first-order differential equation gives the charge on the capacitor at any time t:

**q = C epsilon (1 - e^(-t/RC))** (Eq. 6.22)

The student should verify by substitution that this satisfies Eq. 6.21b. The product **RC** is the **capacitive time constant** of the circuit, with units of time.
- Differentiating q gives the current at any time: **I = dq/dt = (epsilon/R) e^(-t/RC)** (Eq. 6.23), where epsilon/R is the current at t = 0, called I0.

## p.293 - Charging current; discharging equation (printed p.309)
- So **I = I0 e^(-t/RC)** (Eq. 6.24).
- *Fig. 6.18 (graph of q against t, caption: the relation between charge and time when charging a capacitor):* q starts at 0 at t = 0 and rises along a curve that levels off at the value **C epsilon**, reached as t goes to infinity.
- **Discharging:** with the switch S at b in Fig. 6.17, the capacitor discharges through R. The circuit now has no emf (epsilon = 0), and Kirchhoff's second law gives **I R + q/C = 0** (Eq. 6.25). With I = dq/dt (Eq. 6.2): **R dq/dt + q/C = 0** (Eq. 6.26).

## pp.294-295 - Discharging solution (printed pp.310-311)
- The solution is **q = q0 e^(-t/RC)** (Eq. 6.27), where q0 is the initial charge on the capacitor.
- Differentiating: **I = dq/dt = -(q0/RC) e^(-t/RC)** (Eq. 6.28). The **minus sign** shows that the discharge current flows opposite to the charging current, as expected.
- Since q0 = C epsilon (from Eq. 6.22): **I = -(epsilon/R) e^(-t/RC)** (Eq. 6.29), i.e. **I = -I0 e^(-t/RC)** (Eq. 6.30), where epsilon/R = I0 is the current at the start of the discharge.
- *Fig. 6.19 (graph of I against t, caption: current against time when discharging a capacitor, with the vertical axis marked epsilon/R):* the current is largest at t = 0 and then decays **exponentially** with time until it vanishes.

## p.335 - Impedance and phase angle (printed p.361)

**V = I0 R sin(wt) + I0 X_L cos(wt) - I0 X_C cos(wt)**, i.e. **V = I0 [ R sin(wt) + (X_L - X_C) cos(wt) ]** (Eq. 7.18)

- In the term (X_L - X_C), V_L and V_C are **not in phase**; there is a phase angle of **180 degrees** between them.
- Define the **impedance Z** (Arabic: المعاوقة):

**Z = sqrt( R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2 )** (Eq. 7.19)

- Multiplying Eq. 7.18 by Z/Z: **V = I0 Z [ (R/Z) sin(wt) + ((X_L - X_C)/Z) cos(wt) ]** (Eq. 7.20), which can be written **V = I0 Z [ sin(wt) cos(phi) + cos(wt) sin(phi) ]** (Eq. 7.21), where

**cos(phi) = R/Z, sin(phi) = (X_L - X_C)/Z** (printed as a second "7.20"), that is **tan(phi) = (X_L - X_C)/R** (Eq. 7.22).

## p.336 - Ohm's law for ac (printed p.362)
- Eq. 7.21 can be written **V = I0 Z sin(wt + phi)** (Eq. 7.23).
- So the instantaneous current (I = I0 sin(wt)) and the instantaneous voltage (V = I0 Z sin(wt + phi)) are **not in phase**: there is a phase angle phi between them, found from Eq. 7.22.
  - If **X_L > X_C**, phi is **positive** and the **voltage leads the current**.
  - If **X_L < X_C**, phi is **negative** and the **current leads the voltage**.
  - phi is zero if X_L = X_C.
- Writing Eq. 7.23 as V = [I0 sin(wt + phi)] Z gives **V = I Z** (Eq. 7.24), where V and I are the **effective (rms) values** and **Z is the total opposition to the current in the circuit**. Eq. 7.24 is **Ohm's law for ac in the general case**. Equivalently **I = V/Z = V / sqrt(R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2)**.

## p.340 - How a transformer works (printed p.366)
- The primary coil is connected to an **alternating voltage**, so an alternating voltage of the **same frequency** is induced in the secondary. The magnitudes differ because the **numbers of turns** differ. The emf in the primary is V_p = N_p (Delta-phi / Delta-t), where N_p is the number of primary turns and Delta-phi/Delta-t is the rate of change of **magnetic flux**; similarly V_s = N_s (Delta-phi / Delta-t) in the secondary (N_s turns). Dividing:

**V_s = (N_s / N_p) V_p** (Eq. 7.25)

- This relates the **output voltage** to the **input voltage**. For example, with N_s = 100 turns, N_p = 50 turns and an input of 110 V, the output is **220 V**.
- *Fig. 7.11 (caption: the electric transformer):* a rectangular core made of stacked thin iron laminations, with a primary winding N_p on the left limb (input V_p) and a secondary winding N_s on the right limb (output V_s).

## p.341 - 7.7 Electric shock (printed p.367)
- Electric shock is one of the accidents we hear about daily. A current through the body is harmful and may be **fatal**, especially if it passes through the **heart or brain**. The damage depends on the **magnitude, path and direction** of the current.
- A current of **1 mA** can be tolerated; **5 mA** causes pain but is not harmful; a current above about **15 mA** can paralyse the hands and prevent the person from letting go of the source (for instance an electric wire); and **70 mA** passing through a person for one second or more makes the heart muscles beat irregularly (fibrillation), and if this continues the result is **death**.
- Everyday accidents happen when someone touches an uninsulated electric wire: the current flows through the body to the earth. Because the electrical source itself is connected to earth, the person's body becomes the link that **completes the circuit**.
- In such accidents the **first thing to do is open the circuit** (switch off) so that the current stops flowing through the body; then move the victim away from the source using a **dry rope or a dry piece of wood**, and give the victim **artificial respiration** until an ambulance arrives.
